Who waited in line to vote?

This page applies every technique from Modules 2–11 to one question, which is who had to wait in line to vote in the 2014 midterm election. The data come from the Pew Research Center survey taken in the four days after that election, which is the same survey you used in the dispersion module. The notes here are short, and each step links back to its full module if you need a refresher.

The data

Each row is one person who voted in person, either on Election Day or early at a polling place, and told the interviewer whether they had to wait in line. People who voted by mail were not asked. waited is coded 1 = yes, 0 = no, so the mean of waited is simply the share who waited. Keep that in mind, because it's what lets us average, test and regress a yes/no outcome.

Step 3 · Module 6: sampling distributions

Pretend the voters are the population, then sample from them

Treat all 697 in-person voters as the population, where the true share who waited is 21.1%. Draw a random sample of voters, record the share who waited, and then repeat. The sample shares pile up in a bell around the truth, and their spread is the standard error.

Step 4 · Module 7: confidence intervals

Build a confidence interval by hand

The interval is centered on the sample mean. We choose how confident to be, and that choice sets z. The SD and n set the standard error, and everything else is arithmetic.

Step 5 · Module 8: difference in means

Testing who waited

Each test assumes H₀ (no difference) and asks how often a gap this large or larger would show up if H₀ were true. We reject H₀ or fail to reject it, and we never accept anything. Because waited is 0/1, each mean is the share of a group who waited.

Read first

Regression on a 0/1 outcome goes beyond what we cover in class

In class, our regression DVs are interval-level, like feeling thermometers or vote shares. Waiting in line is only 0 or 1. The mean of a 0/1 variable is a proportion, so the mean of waited is simply the share of voters who waited, and the poll shares in Module 7 work the same way. Running ordinary least squares on a 0/1 DV has a name, the linear probability model, and Steps 6–8 use it on purpose as extra illustration on voters you already know. In class and on the labs, regression DVs stay interval-level.

When do social scientists use OLS with binary DVs?

What changes when Y is 0/1

The mean of a 0/1 variable is a proportion, so a predicted value ŷ reads as a predicted probability, and a slope reads as a change in percentage points. With a single dummy IV, the slope equals the difference in means from Step 5, exactly.

When researchers use it

  • When they want effects that are easy to read and compare, such as “urban voters were 12 points more likely to wait than rural voters.”
  • Often in economics and political science, especially for experiments and models built mostly of dummies (did a get-out-the-vote mailer raise turnout?). Angrist and Pischke's Mostly Harmless Econometrics (2009) is a well-known defense.
  • As a first look before a model built for 0/1 outcomes.

Its limits

  • Impossible predictions. A straight line can run above 1 or below 0. In Step 8 the lowest line comes close to 0 for the oldest voters.
  • Bent reality. Near 0 and 1, the true relationship usually curves. Models built for 0/1 outcomes bend the line to follow that curve, and they come in later courses.
  • Standard errors. When Y can only be 0 or 1, the errors can't spread evenly, so published work usually reports “robust” standard errors. Here they barely change anything, so the page shows the ordinary ones you know.

How to read Steps 6–8

Interpret every slope as a change in the chance of waiting, in percentage points, and every prediction as a probability. The p-value logic is unchanged, since under H₀ the slope is 0.

The South, holding community type constant

Step 8 · Module 11: dummy variables Linear probability model

Community dummies, then community, region and age together

Community type has three categories, so it gets two dummies. The category left out is the reference, so the constant is its level and each coefficient is a gap from it. Then we add the South dummy and age, and each dummy shifts a line up or down, which gives six parallel lines, one for each combination of community type and region.

Check yourself

The mean of waited is .211. What does that number mean?

21.1% of in-person voters had to wait in line. For a 0/1 variable, the mean is the share coded 1.

Minutes waited has a mean of 26.0 and a median of 10. Which describes the typical wait?

The median does. Six voters reported waits of four to five hours, and that long right tail drags the mean far above the typical wait.

Urban vs. rural voters: Sig. = .004. What do you conclude?

Reject H₀. If urban and rural voters waited equally often, a gap this large or larger would turn up about 4 times in 1,000. Urban voters waited at 25.1% and rural voters at 12.6%, so the gap runs the way H₁ predicted.

Early in-person voters vs. Election Day voters: Sig. = .834. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. The two groups waited at 20.4% and 21.2%, and a gap that small would turn up often by chance. That isn't evidence the groups waited equally, only that there is not enough evidence of a difference.

The slope on south is .057. Where have you seen that number before?

You saw it in Step 5, where it is the share of Southern voters who waited (.244) minus the share of everyone else (.187). A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test, and it gives the same t and p.

The South's slope is .057 (Sig. = .070) alone but .067 (Sig. = .034) holding community type and age constant. Why?

Southern in-person voters were more often rural, and rural voters waited least. Leaving community type out let the South borrow rural voters' short lines, which hid part of the South's own effect.

Sources and data
Data: Pew Research Center, 2014 post-election survey, interviews conducted November 6–9, 2014 (2014ElectionSurvey_F26.sav). The page uses the 697 respondents who voted in person, on Election Day or early, and answered whether they had to wait in line (q9). People who voted by mail were not asked. Studies comparing linear and logistic regression find the two usually agree on the direction and significance of effects when the outcome's probabilities stay roughly between 20% and 80%, and agree less when the outcome is rare (Hellevik 2009; von Hippel 2017; Gomila 2021).

Formulas

Confidence interval for a mean

x‾±z×SDn

z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.58 (99%). For a 0/1 variable, x̄ is a proportion.

Lambda

λ=E1−E2E1

E₁ counts the errors from guessing the DV's mode for everyone, and E₂ counts the errors from guessing each IV column's mode.

Gamma

γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd

Nₛ counts pairs ranked the same way on both variables, and N_d counts pairs ranked in opposite ways. Ties are ignored.

Difference in means (equal variances)

t=x‾1−x‾2SDp1n1+1n2

SDₚ is the pooled SD of the two groups. H₀ says the difference is 0.

Linear probability model

P^(waited)=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+constant

This is ordinary least squares with a 0/1 DV. Each β is a change in the probability of waiting (× 100 for percentage points), holding the other IVs constant.
