Before we start · Hypotheses

What are we testing?

Now that you know how to build confidence intervals, and what they are capturing, we can turn that concept into a way to rigorously test hypotheses. We will define two meaningful groups using a second variable in our data, compare the means of those two groups, and ask whether the difference between them is statistically significant.

H₁ · the research hypothesis

The two groups differ. For example, women were more likely to survive the Titanic disaster than men.

H₀ · the null hypothesis

There is no difference between the two groups. For example, there is no difference in the likelihood of surviving between men and women.

Recall that we assume the truth of the null hypothesis and ask if we can reject it. Every panel on this page builds toward one question.

“In a world where there is truly no difference between the two groups, how frequently would we obtain the difference in means we find?”

If the answer is rarely, fewer than 5 times in 100 when α = .05, we reject H₀. Otherwise we fail to reject it. Either way the decision is about H₀, and we never accept either hypothesis.

Reading a p-value

    
Every test asks the same question. If H₀ were true and nothing were really going on, how often would chance alone give us a difference this large or larger? The p-value is that “how often.”

    
Recall the jelly bean bowl in Module 6, where you rated how surprised you would be by a handful. A p-value puts a number on that same surprise. We imagine a world where H₀ is true, and we ask how often a result like ours would turn up there. A large p means our result would be ordinary in that world, so it is not surprising and we fail to reject H₀. A small p means our result would rarely turn up in a world with no difference, which is surprising enough that we reject H₀ and conclude the difference is plausibly real rather than random.

  • Match your wording to the sign. A p of .05 means about 5 times in 100, while p < .05 means fewer than 5 times in 100.
    • 
      
  • There are only two possible decisions, and both are about H₀. If p < α we reject H₀, and if p ≥ α we fail to reject H₀. We never “accept” anything, because failing to reject means the evidence was not strong enough, which is different from showing the groups are the same.
    • 
      
  • p is not the chance that H₀ is true. It's the chance of a result like ours if H₀ were true.
    • 
      
  • The .05 cutoff (α) is a convention we choose rather than a law of nature. Set α to .01 above and the bar gets higher.
    • 
      
  • The same logic applies in regression, where H₀ says the slope is 0 and p is how often a slope at least this steep would turn up by chance.

Check yourself

      

        The two groups' 95% CIs overlap a little. Can the difference still be significant?
        
Yes. The standard error of a difference is √(SE₁² + SE₂²), which is smaller than SE₁ + SE₂. So two intervals can overlap while the interval for the difference excludes zero. If the intervals do not overlap at all, the difference is significant, but if they overlap, you need to test the difference directly.

      

      

        H₀ is true. At α = .05, how often will the test reject?
        
About 5% of the time. That is what α means, since it is the false-positive (Type I error) rate you choose in advance. Turn on “Make H₀ true” to check.

      

      

        The true gap is the same at n = 25 and n = 250. Why does the test reject far more often at 250?
        
The standard error shrinks with √n, so the distribution of differences tightens around the true gap and pulls away from zero. The share of samples that reject is the test's power.

      

      

        p = .03. Is there a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true?
        
No. p is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if H₀ were true. It is a statement about the data given H₀ rather than about H₀ given the data.

      

      

        When should you drop “Assume equal variances”?
        
Drop it when the groups' standard deviations differ noticeably, as they do with the drop in turnout (governor-race states 4.2, Senate-race states 5.1). The Welch version, Stata's unequal option, is the safer default. With equal group sizes the two versions give nearly identical answers.

      

    

  


  
  
Wrap-up
What we learned

  

    
    
      
  • A difference-in-means test asks whether the gap between two group means in our sample is larger than chance alone would produce if the groups were really the same (the null hypothesis, H₀).
    • 
      
  • The t statistic counts how many standard errors the gap sits away from zero.
    • 
      
  • The p-value is how often chance alone would give a gap this large or larger. If p is below .05 we reject H₀, and otherwise we fail to reject it, which is not the same as showing H₀ is true.
    • 
      
  • Rejecting a true H₀ is a Type I error, and failing to reject a false one is a Type II error. A stricter α makes the first rarer and the second more common.
    • 
    

  


  
Sources and data

    Data: 2026 news-desert and turnout data for 2,709 counties in 38 states (newsdeserts_turnout2026.sav, built for this course in November 2026). Local news outlets per county come from the Medill State of Local News Project's 2026 report. 2026 votes are AP county results as published by NBC News, counted for the governor's race where there was one and otherwise the U.S. Senate race, and they are unofficial counts as of Nov. 25, 2026, before states certified. Turnout divides those votes by the citizen voting-age population from the American Community Survey, 2019–2023, and 2024 turnout uses Tony McGovern's county presidential results.
    The full set of counties is treated as the population, and samples are drawn from it without replacement.
    “Make H₀ true” pools both groups and draws each sample from the pool, so the true difference is exactly zero.

Formulas

  

    
The estimate and the null hypothesis

    x‾1−x‾2H0:μ1−μ2=0
  

  

    
Standard error of the difference (unequal variances)

    SE=SD12n1+SD22n2=SE12+SE22
    
This is always smaller than SE₁ + SE₂, which is why overlapping CIs can hide a significant difference.

  

  

    
Pooled version (equal variances)

    SDp2=(n1−1)SD12+(n2−1)SD22n1+n2−2SE=SDp1n1+1n2
    
Degrees of freedom: n₁ + n₂ − 2.

  

  

    
Test statistic and interval

    t=(x‾1−x‾2)−0SE(x‾1−x‾2)±t*·SE
    
Reject H₀ at level α when |t| exceeds the critical value t*, which is exactly when the (1 − α) interval for the difference excludes zero.

  

  

    
Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom

    df=(SD12/n1+SD22/n2)2(SD12/n1)2n1−1+(SD22/n2)2n2−1
    
We use this when variances are not assumed equal, and it is usually not a whole number.

  

  

    
Effect size (Cohen's d)

    d=μ1−μ2σ
    
Cohen's d is the gap between the groups in SD units. Roughly, 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium and 0.8 is large, and a tiny d can still be “significant” with a big enough n.
