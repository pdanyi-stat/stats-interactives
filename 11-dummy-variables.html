● Module 11 · Regression

Dummy variables.

Regression needs numbers, but region, party and gender are categories. A dummy variable turns a category into a 0/1 switch. Its coefficient is a difference between groups, and in a regression with other IVs it shifts the line up or down.

Part 1 · Dummies you have already met

You have used dummies in almost every technique so far

A dummy variable is any variable with two categories coded 0 and 1, such as survived (1) or died (0), or woman (1) or man (0). The name is new in this module, but the idea is not, since two-category variables have run through the site from the start. Here are a few of the places they showed up, so you can see that the regressions below rest on things you already know.

Module 2 · Central tendency

The mean of a dummy is a share

.382

Code each Titanic passenger 1 if they survived and 0 if they died. The mean of that variable is 500 / 1,309 = .382, which is the share of passengers who survived. That is why a 0/1 variable is the one case where the mode, the median and the mean all work.

Module 4b · Crosstabs

A crosstab of two dummies

73% vs. 19%

Sex and survival are both dummies, so the Titanic crosstab is a 2 × 2 table. The column percentage for survived is the mean of the survived dummy within each column, so 73% of the 466 women survived and 19% of the 843 men did.

Module 5 · Lambda

Lambda with a two-column IV

λ = 0

Gender is a nominal dummy, so lambda fits the 2024 presidential vote by gender. Harris won 60% of the women and 51% of the men in that table, yet λ = 0 because Harris is the best guess in both columns.

Module 5 · Gamma

Gamma, where the coding sets the sign

γ = +.837 or −.837

Two categories can always be put in order, so gamma works with a dummy too. Code female = 1 and survived = 1, and the Titanic table gives γ = +.837. Code male = 1 instead, and the same table gives −.837, so the strength stays the same while the sign follows the coding.

Module 8 · Difference in means

Every t test splits the data with a dummy

50.7 vs. 47.9

A difference in means needs two groups, and a dummy defines them. In the 2026 elections, counties with two or more local news outlets turned out 50.7% of their citizens of voting age, and counties with one outlet turned out 47.9%, which is a gap of 2.9 points. Part 2 shows that a regression on a dummy returns that kind of gap as its slope.

Part 2 · One dummy

A dummy is a 0/1 switch, and its slope is a difference in means

Code races with a Republican incumbent = 1 and every other race = 0, and then run an ordinary regression.Code South = 1 and every other state = 0, and then run an ordinary regression. With only two possible x values, the least-squares line passes through the two group means. The constant is the mean of the 0 group, the slope is the gap between the groups, and the run is always exactly 1.

A regression on one dummy matches the t test

The highlighted numbers are the same in both outputs, because a regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test from Module 8.


        

      

    

  


  
  

    
Part 3 · Dummy plus a continuous IV

    
A dummy shifts the line up or down, giving parallel lines

    
Add the district's Cook PVI as a second IV. Plug in rinc = 0 and you get the line for other seats, and plug in rinc = 1 and the same line moves up or down by the rinc coefficient. The lines share a slope but have different intercepts. The model assumes the incumbent gap is the same at every PVI, which is why the two lines are parallel.

    
Add metro share as a second IV. Plug in south = 0 and you get the non-South line, and plug in south = 1 and the same line moves up or down by the South coefficient. The lines share a slope but have different intercepts. The model assumes the regional gap is the same at every metro share, which is why the two lines are parallel.

Part 4 · More than two categories

    
More than two categories need one dummy per region except the reference

    
A variable with k categories gets k − 1 dummies. The category left out is the reference, so the constant is its mean and every coefficient is a gap from it. Change the reference and the coefficients change, but the predictions don't.

  

  



  
Reading p for a coefficient. H₀ says the coefficient is 0, and p is how often a coefficient at least this far from 0 would turn up by chance if H₀ were true. So p = .03 means about 3 times in 100, and Stata's 0.000 (SPSS prints .000) means p < .001, or fewer than 1 time in 1,000. If p < .05 we reject H₀, and otherwise we fail to reject it. The .05 cutoff is a convention we choose, and it sets our risk of a Type I error (a false positive). Reading p-values → · Type I and II errors →

1 · A coefficient is a gap, not a level

        
With the Northeast as the reference, the constant is the Northeast's average, which is % in your version, and each coefficient is how far a region's average sits above or below it. Your Midwest averages % and your West averages %, so their coefficients are 0 and +5 even though neither region's own average is anywhere near 0 or 5.

        

        
2 · Two ways to reach the goal

        
Moving the Midwest or the West changes only that region's coefficient. Moving the Northeast moves the baseline itself, so it changes every coefficient at once while the other regions' averages stay put. 

        
3 · Back to the lesson

        
When you read a dummy coefficient in output, say what it is a gap from. A Midwest coefficient of 0 means Midwestern races average the same Democratic share as Northeastern ones, and it doesn't mean Democrats drew no votes in the Midwest.

Check yourself

      

        The constant in ŷ = −21.36·rinc + 65.49 is 65.49. What is it?
        
It is the predicted Democratic share when rinc = 0, which is the mean for races without a Republican incumbent. With a dummy-only model the constant is always the reference group's mean.

      

      

        In the parallel-lines model, the rinc coefficient is about −2.5. Interpret it.
        
Holding PVI constant, Democrats running against a Republican incumbent are predicted to win about 2.5 fewer points of the two-party vote than Democrats in other races. Because the lines are parallel, that gap is the same at every PVI.

      

      

        The rinc coefficient is −21.4 in Part 2 but only −2.5 in Part 3. Why?
        
Part 3 holds PVI constant. Republican incumbents mostly hold Republican-leaning districts (an average PVI of about R+12, against about D+8 for the other seats), and PVI strongly predicts the Democratic share. Leaving PVI out let the district's lean pose as an incumbency effect. Bivariate gap = gap holding PVI constant + (PVI's slope × the difference in PVI).

      

      

        The constant in ŷ = 4.32·South + 47.56 is 47.56. What is it?
        
It is the predicted Trump share when South = 0, which is the mean for non-Southern states. With a dummy-only model the constant is always the reference group's mean.

      

      

        In the parallel-lines model, the South coefficient is about 5.6. Interpret it.
        
Holding metro share constant, Southern states are predicted to give Trump about 5.6 more points than non-Southern states. Because the lines are parallel, that gap is the same at every metro share.

      

      

        The South coefficient is 4.3 in Part 2 but 5.6 in Part 3. Why?
        
Part 3 holds metro share constant. Southern states are a bit more metropolitan on average (by about 3 points with D.C., 2 without), and metro share pulls Trump's share down. Leaving metro share out let that hide part of the regional gap. Bivariate gap = gap holding metro constant + (metro's slope × the difference in metro share).

      

      

        Why not include a dummy for every region?
        
Every case is in exactly one region, so the four dummies always add up to 1. That is identical to the constant, so the model can't separate them (perfect collinearity, the “dummy variable trap”). Leave one category out as the reference.

      

      

        You change the reference from Northeast to South. What changes and what doesn't?
        
The constant and every coefficient change, because they are now gaps from the South. Predicted values, R², and the overall F test do not change, because it is the same model described from a different starting point.

      

    

  


  
  
Wrap-up
What we learned

  

    
    
      
  • A dummy variable is coded 0 or 1, and its slope is the difference in the mean of Y between the two groups.
    • 
      
  • Adding a dummy to a model with an interval IV shifts the line up or down for one group, which gives two parallel lines.
    • 
      
  • A variable with several categories needs one dummy per category except the reference group.
    • 
      
  • Switching the reference group changes what the slopes compare but not the predictions.

Formulas

  
One dummy

    y^=β1D+constantconstant=y‾D=0,β1=y‾D=1−y‾D=0
    
Its t test is the equal-variance two-sample t test.

  
Dummy plus a continuous IV (parallel lines)

    y^=β1X+β2D+constant
    
D = 0: ŷ = β₁X + constant. D = 1: ŷ = β₁X + (constant + β₂). The slope stays the same, and the intercept shifts by β₂. β₂ is the gap between groups holding X constant, and the model assumes that gap is the same at every value of X.

  
k categories

    y^=β1D1+⋯+βk−1Dk−1+constant
    
The constant is the mean of the reference category, and βj is the mean of category j minus the reference mean. In Stata, i.region builds the dummies and ib2.region makes level 2 the reference.
