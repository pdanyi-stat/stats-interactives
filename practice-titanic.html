The data

Step 1 · Labs: central tendency, dispersion, skew

Describe the passengers

A variable's level of measurement decides which average makes sense. For interval-ratio variables, skew decides whether the mean still sits in the middle.

Step 2 · Module 6: sampling distributions

Pretend the ship is the population, then sample from it

Treat all 1,309 passengers as the population, where the true survival rate is 38.2%. Draw a random sample of passengers, record its survival rate, and then repeat. The sample rates pile up in a bell around the truth, and their spread is the standard error.

Step 3 · Module 7: confidence intervals

Build a confidence interval by hand

The interval is centered on the sample mean. We choose how confident to be, and that choice sets z. The SD and n set the standard error. Everything else is arithmetic.

Step 4 · Module 5: crosstabs, lambda and gamma

Does knowing X help you guess who survived?

Survival (the DV) runs down the rows and the IV runs across the columns, with column percentages. Lambda fits nominal IVs, and gamma fits ordered ones. The strength labels follow the scales from lab.

Step 5 · Module 8: difference in means

Testing the lab's questions

Each test assumes H₀ (no difference) and asks how often a gap this large or larger would show up if H₀ were true. We reject H₀ or fail to reject it, and we never accept anything. Because survived is 0/1, each mean is a survival rate.

Read first

Regression on a 0/1 outcome goes beyond what we cover in class

In class, our regression DVs are interval-level, like feeling thermometers or vote shares. Survival is only 0 or 1. Running ordinary least squares on a 0/1 DV has a name, the linear probability model, and Steps 6–8 use it on purpose as extra illustration on passengers you already know. In class and on the labs, regression DVs stay interval-level.

When do social scientists use OLS with binary DVs?

What changes when Y is 0/1

The mean of a 0/1 variable is a proportion, so a predicted value ŷ reads as a predicted probability, and a slope reads as a change in percentage points. With a single dummy IV, the slope equals the difference in means from Step 5, exactly.

When researchers use it

  • When they want effects that are easy to read and compare, such as “women were 54 points more likely to survive.”
  • Often in economics and political science, especially for experiments and models built mostly of dummies (did a get-out-the-vote mailer raise turnout?). Angrist and Pischke's Mostly Harmless Econometrics (2009) is a well-known defense.
  • As a first look before a model built for 0/1 outcomes.

Its limits

  • Impossible predictions. A straight line can run above 1 or below 0. You'll see this in Step 8.
  • Bent reality. Near 0 and 1, the true relationship usually curves. Logit and probit models build that curve in, and you'll meet them in later courses.
  • Standard errors. When Y can only be 0 or 1, the errors can't spread evenly, so published work usually reports “robust” standard errors. Here they barely change anything, so the page shows the ordinary ones you know.

How to read Steps 6–8

Interpret every slope as a change in the chance of surviving, in percentage points, and every prediction as a probability. The p-value logic is unchanged, since under H₀ the slope is 0.

Step 6 · Module 9: bivariate regression Linear probability model

One IV at a time

We start with a dummy IV (female) and then a continuous one (age). The equation is the same either way, y = mx + b, or in stats notation f(x) = β₁X₁ + constant.

Step 7 · Module 10: multiple regression Linear probability model

Age, holding class constant

On its own, age barely predicts survival. Add controls and watch its slope change. All four models use the same 1,046 passengers whose age is known, so the comparison is fair.

Step 8 · Module 11: dummy variables Linear probability model

Class dummies, then sex, class and age together

Class has three categories, so it gets two dummies. The class left out is the reference, so the constant is its level and each coefficient is a gap from it. Then we add sex and age, and each dummy shifts a line up or down, which gives six parallel lines, one for each combination of sex and class.

Try this

  1. In Step 1, compare the mean and median of siblings or spouses. Which better describes the typical passenger, and why?
  2. In Step 2, draw 1,000 samples at n = 10, then at n = 100. How much narrower is the bell? (√10 ≈ 3.2.)
  3. In Step 3, pick First class and switch from 95% to 99%. Which numbers in the hand calculation change?
  4. In Step 4, pick Traveling alone. The survival rates differ by 20 points, yet λ is almost 0. Why?
  5. In Step 5, test lower vs. upper class. You reject H₀, but does the result support H₁?
  6. In Step 8a, switch the reference class from Lower to First. Which numbers change, and which stay the same?
  7. In Step 8b, build a 2-year-old girl in first class, then a 75-year-old man in lower class. What goes wrong?

Check yourself

The mean of survived is .382. What does that number mean?

38.2% of passengers survived. For a 0/1 variable, the mean is the share coded 1.

λ = .42 for sex and survival. Interpret it.

Knowing a passenger's sex cuts our errors in guessing survival by 42% (from 500 errors to 288). On the lab scale, .40 and up is strong.

Youths vs. adults: p = .405. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. If there were no real difference, a gap this large or larger would show up about 41 times in 100. That isn't evidence the groups were the same, only that there is not enough evidence of a difference.

Lower vs. upper class: Sig. = .000. Does that support “the lower class survived at a greater rate”?

No. We reject H₀, but the gap runs the other way, since the lower class survived at 25.5% and the upper class at 61.9%. Always check the direction against H₁.

The slope on female is .536. Where have you seen that number before?

You saw it in Step 5, where it is women's survival rate (.728) minus men's (.191). A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test, and it gives the same t and p.

Age's slope is −.0019 (p = .07) alone but −.0077 (p < .001) holding class constant. Why?

Older passengers were concentrated in first class, which survived far more often. Leaving class out let age borrow first class's advantage, which hid age's own negative effect.

The model predicts over 100% for a 2-year-old girl in first class. What's wrong?

A straight line has no ceiling. That's a known limit of the linear probability model, and logit and probit keep predictions between 0 and 1.

Crew are not included. This list is nearly every passenger rather than a random sample; like the labs, the page treats it as a sample to practice inference. Formulas

Confidence interval for a mean

x‾±z×SDn

z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.58 (99%). For a 0/1 variable, x̄ is a proportion.

Lambda

λ=E1−E2E1

E₁: errors guessing the DV's mode for everyone. E₂: errors guessing each IV column's mode.

Gamma

γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd

Nₛ counts pairs ranked the same way on both variables, and N_d counts pairs ranked in opposite ways. Ties are ignored.

Difference in means (equal variances)

t=x‾1−x‾2SDp1n1+1n2

SDₚ is the pooled SD of the two groups. H₀: the difference is 0.

Linear probability model

P^(survived)=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+constant

This is ordinary least squares with a 0/1 DV. Each β is a change in the probability of surviving (× 100 for percentage points), holding the other IVs constant.
