Bivariate regression

One line summarizes how Y moves with X. Fit it, read it as an equation, use it to make predictions, and ask whether its slope is steeper than chance alone would produce.

Worked example: Partisans and the First Lady (2004 NES feeling thermometers), a step-by-step lab companion.

BReading the equation

Algebra class
y = mx + b

m is the slope: rise over run. b is the intercept: where the line crosses x = 0.

Statistics
ŷ = β₁X₁ + constant

Same line, new names. β₁ is the coefficient on the IV; the constant (Stata: _cons, often written β₀) is the intercept. The hat on ŷ means “predicted.”

Check yourself

      

        The slope on metro share is about −0.37. Interpret it.
        
Each 1-point increase in the share of a state's population living in metro areas is associated with about a 0.37-point decrease in Trump's predicted 2016 vote share. “Associated with,” not “causes”: this is one variable, observational data.

      

      

        The constant is 77. Should we predict that a state with no metro residents would give Trump 77%?
        
No. The lowest metro share in the data is 31%, so x = 0 is far outside the observed range. The constant pins the line in place; it is only a meaningful prediction when x = 0 is a realistic value.

      

      

        Why does the line always pass through (x̄, ȳ)?
        
Because the constant is computed as ȳ − β₁x̄. Plug x̄ into the equation and you get exactly ȳ. That is why panel D can pivot every line around the mean point.

      

      

        Stata reports t = b / SE. What does a t of about −4.9 mean in panel D?
        
The real slope is almost five standard errors away from zero: far out in the sideways bell, well outside the wedge. A slope that steep would almost never come from shuffled, unrelated data, so p is tiny.

      

      

        Can a line be steep but not significant?
        
Yes. Significance depends on the slope relative to its standard error. With few cases or lots of scatter, the bell of chance slopes is wide, and even a steep line can fall inside the wedge.

Formulas

  
The model

    y^=β1X1+constant⟺y=mx+b
    
Many textbooks write the constant as β₀ and put it first: ŷ = β₀ + β₁X₁.

  
Least-squares slope and constant

    β1=∑(xi−x‾)(yi−y‾)∑(xi−x‾)2constant=y‾−β1x‾
    
These values minimize the sum of squared residuals, SSE = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)².

  
Predicted change (marginal effect)

    Δy^=β1·Δx
    
For a straight line the change depends only on how far X moves, not where it starts. Rise = slope × run.

  
Fit

    r=∑(xi−x‾)(yi−y‾)∑(xi−x‾)2∑(yi−y‾)2r2=1−SSESST

  
Standard error of the slope and the t test

    SE(β1)=SSE/(n−2)∑(xi−x‾)2t=β1−0SE(β1)
    
More scatter around the line (bigger SSE) widens the bell of chance slopes; more spread in X narrows it. Under H₀: β₁ = 0, t follows a t distribution with n − 2 degrees of freedom. 95% CI: β₁ ± t*·SE.

  
Why the bell lands on the right edge

    yedge=y‾+b·(xedge−x‾)
    
Each line through (x̄, ȳ) reaches the right edge at a height that is a straight-line function of its slope b. So the distribution of slopes becomes a distribution of heights on that edge: the same bell curve, turned 90°.
