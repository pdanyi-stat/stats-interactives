Asking research questions.

Research in political science should be relevant to contemporary problems and politics, and it should interest you, the researcher, as well as a larger audience such as the public, other political scientists or policymakers. A good question is interesting and leads to conclusions that might be useful. This page practices two routes to a question, one that starts from what is normal and one that starts from what is odd, and then turns to the topics you care about.

[DRAFT ONLY] This page is adapted from the course exercise on generating research questions, and its parts may still change.

Part 1

Start from what is normal

One route to a good research question is to begin with the mundane or normal and ask what led to it, or why it is the way it is. When things are normal, they are likely to reflect, or be the outcome of, bigger patterns in the political world, so the answer is likely to be a general one. But first we must go from an observation to a question about politics.

Crossing from North Carolina into Tennessee

Driving from North Carolina to Tennessee presents an interesting observation. Just about every road you take across the border becomes noticeably worse once you enter Tennessee. Because worse roads on one side of a state line are normal, a pattern we see again and again, the answer is likely to be related to something general about how states work.

In class we do this in groups of three or four, and we give it four minutes. Write as many why, how or what questions about this pattern as you can.

  • Why would the quality of a road change so sharply at a state line?
  • How do North Carolina and Tennessee each pay for the roads they maintain?
  • What does each state spend on a mile of road, and who decides how much?
  • Does the way a state raises money, such as whether it taxes income, shape how much it spends on roads?
  • Which level of government, the state or the county, is responsible for rural roads in each state?

Part 2

Then look for what is odd

Another way to generate a good research question is to look at the odd and particular and ask what led to it, or why it doesn't fit the pattern. These observations are a bit harder to find, because you first have to pay attention to patterns and hold strong expectations for the way things are supposed to be.

Badges on old tombstones

If you look closely at some tombstones, you'll see the badges or symbols of organizations such as the Freemasons or the Knights of Columbus carved into the stone. Because these little badges are not common today, something must be different today, and that difference must be related to some change over time. Again, the first step is to go from an observation to a question about politics.

In groups of three or four, take four minutes and write as many why, how or what questions about tombstones and their little badges as you can.

  • Why did so many people once want their membership in a fraternal group carved on their grave?
  • What happened to membership in groups like the Freemasons over the twentieth century?
  • How did belonging to these organizations connect people to local politics and civic life?
  • Do places where these groups faded also see less civic participation today?

Part 3

From questions to consequences

Next, attempt to identify some important consequences related to the questions you've identified, about either the roads at the state line or the badges on tombstones. A question earns its place when the answer would tell us something about how politics shapes people's lives.

  • If worse roads follow from how a state raises money, then tax choices made in a state capitol show up in commute times, freight costs and crash rates for the people who live there.
  • If the groups behind the tombstone badges once pulled people into civic life, their decline could help explain why fewer people take part in local politics today.

Part 4

Which route does an observation call for?

Each card below is an observation, and most come from data used elsewhere on this site. Decide whether it is a normal pattern, where we would ask what produces it, or an odd case, where we would ask why it breaks the pattern. Then compare your call with ours and read a question that route might lead to.

Part 5

Your own topics and questions

Let's shift to topics and observations you are personally interested in and have observed. Recall that topics are often too large to use as your only starting point for a research project, and that forming narrow questions makes a project easier to manage and complete, so complete this part on your own.

Step 1 · Three topics

Identify three topics in politics you have an interest in, such as war, terrorism, Congress, or gender and race.

Step 2 · What you have observed

For each topic, write (a) something you have observed within it that is normal, a pattern you see, and (b) something particular that seems odd or out of the ordinary for that topic.

Step 3 · Questions from each observation

Generate a research question for each observation above. Some won't be great, and that's fine, but try asking what, why or how about each one. Be sure that your questions are not normative and can be answered, meaning that answering them is achievable. A quick check appears under each question as you type.

Step 4 · The one with the most promise

If one question has the most promise for a research proposal this semester, tick the box under it so it stands out in your download.

Wrap-up

What we learned

  • A research question should be relevant to politics today and interesting to you and to a wider audience.
  • One route starts from something normal and asks what produces the pattern, and another starts from something odd and asks why it breaks the pattern.
  • A topic such as Congress is too large to start from, so we narrow it to a question about something we have observed.
  • Good research questions ask why, how or what, they aren't normative, and they can be answered with evidence.
