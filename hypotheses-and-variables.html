Hypotheses and variables.

Statistical tests start from a pair of hypotheses about two variables. This page covers what a hypothesis needs, which variable is the cause, and which way a relationship runs.

Part 1

What a hypothesis needs

A hypothesis formalizes what we expect to see when we study the political world. A workable one has four parts:

  • It suggests a relationship between two things.
  • It can suggest a direction for that relationship. A direction is preferred when you have a reason to expect one, but when you don't, saying only that two things are related is fine.
  • Hypotheses come in a pair: a research hypothesis (H₁) and a null hypothesis (H₀).
  • The null hypothesis always states that there is no relationship between the two things.

An example. Some countries choose their leaders through millions of voters, while others use a small circle of generals or party officials. Bueno de Mesquita and his co-authors argue that leaders chosen by a large group can't afford to buy off everyone they need, so they provide public goods instead, while leaders chosen by a small group can keep power by rewarding a few insiders (Bueno de Mesquita et al. 2003). That argument becomes a pair of hypotheses:

H₁: The larger the number of people selecting a leader in a political system, the less likely that system is to display corruption.

H₀: There is no relationship between the number of people selecting a leader and corruption.

Part 2

Which one is the independent variable?

The independent variable (IV, or X) is the one we think does the causing. The dependent variable (DV, or Y) is the outcome. To tell them apart, ask which one could change the other. A cause has to come first in time, so something that happens later can't change something that was already set.

Sometimes the order isn't obvious, and either variable could plausibly affect the other. Then researchers have to argue for an order, and readers can disagree.

Part 3

Positive or inverse

“Positive” means the two variables change in the same direction, so both go up together or both go down together. “Inverse” (or negative) means they move in opposite directions, so one goes up as the other goes down. Positive does not mean good.

Watch how a variable is coded. On the Titanic, first class is coded 1 and third class is coded 3, so a higher number means a lower class. Whether a relationship comes out positive or inverse depends on those codes, so a graph needs axis labels with the values on each scale before you can say which way it runs.

A hypothesis can also say only that two variables are related, without naming a direction. That's fine when you have no reason to expect one, but state the direction when you do.
