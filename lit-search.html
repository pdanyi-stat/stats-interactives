Finding the scholarly debate.

As you settle on a research question, the next step in developing your project and a proposal is to identify what prior research on your question has to say. To accomplish this you must do three main things, which are to find the prior work on your question, to filter out the work that is most relevant and important, and to understand the competing arguments made in that work. This page takes up the three tasks step by step, so you can practice the skills it takes to do them efficiently.

Part 1

Turn your question into search terms

Most students begin with library search databases such as EBSCO and JSTOR, or with something more general like Google Scholar. At this early stage, don't rely too heavily on search databases alone. They tend to give preference to research articles, which are great but often rather narrow in scope, and the long list of results makes it difficult to tell which of the many articles are really the ones you want. So we do search databases, but we use them as quick first steps aimed at finding one or two articles on the topic, and then we move on to a second phase of searching.

Think of the search as a time machine

It helps to think of the whole search as a trip through the literature in a time machine. The search box only gets the trip started, and the goal is to get away from it as quickly as you can. Once a search has turned up one credible work on your question, we stop typing terms and let that work carry us through the literature instead.

  • To go backwards in time, we follow the citations in a published work's literature review back to the earlier works it builds on.
  • To go forward in time, we use Google Scholar's “Cited by” link to find the later works that cite it.

Each work we find this way has its own literature review and its own “Cited by” link, so we repeat the loop and travel backwards and forwards from each new work. We return to the search box only once these loops stop turning up new and useful work, and by then we usually know better search terms for the next round. Part 2 takes the trip backwards in time, and Part 4 takes it forward.

Your question and six search terms

To use search databases, you need to convert your research question into search terms that target the kinds of work related to it. Typing the question itself is unlikely to prove useful, so boil it down to concrete terms that, alone or in combination, relate to your question and topic. If you are unsure of the key terms scholars use, take a glance at an introductory textbook and see which terms it singles out. If you are still unsure, asking someone with more expertise is another good way to get started.

Part 2

A worked example on why the poor vote less

JSTOR is a scholarly database that covers a wide range of topics, including much of the work in political science, and you can reach it from Booth Library's homepage. Suppose our research question is why the poor, or people with lower social and economic status, vote less. A search for the very general term “voting” returns a wide variety of works related to voting or merely mentioning it, and with such a broad term it would take a good deal of luck to land right on the debate over our question. Since our aim is still to find just one or two articles directly related to our topic, here is how JSTOR's tools help narrow the results.

Narrow the search, then find one solid article

Use the Search within results box to search only inside the results of your search for “voting.” Here we used it to search for “poverty,” and the results from narrowing the search inch us closer to the one solid article we need.

We are interested in that first result. It may seem only tangentially related to why the poor vote or don't, but it is close enough that its author will have addressed the debate over the poor and voting in the paper's own literature review, and that is what we are after. We are also interested in it because it appears in a reputable journal, which raises our confidence that the author has done a good job of situating the work in the debate over voting in general, and over voting among people facing challenges more specifically. JSTOR indexes only peer-reviewed journals of some reasonable credibility, so we can partly rely on that.

Go backwards in time through the article's citations

When we open the article and begin to read, we find largely what we are looking for, a general statement of the question about voting and who votes less, along with a few general citations to research on that question.

The citations to Wolfinger and Rosenstone and to Verba, Schlozman and Brady are what we are after. The author cites them prominently, in the opening of the article and in support of a big claim about who takes part less, and that tells us a great deal. An experienced scholar who knows the field is telling us which research matters and which works are part of the conversation on our question. It is now highly likely that these works are fairly important, influential and informative on the debate over why some groups vote less, so they are likely to address the poor and voting as well. What we are doing here is letting smart, experienced people tell us what research matters, so that they do some of the work of finding the debate for us. Asking faculty who know the field does the same thing, and it is often the quickest way in. As someone new to a topic, it is extremely useful to rely on experts to point out where the debate over a question lies.

Our next step is to verify that these works are on the topic and look like good general works on this question, and their place in the bibliography suggests they are fairly general. Both are books, which is good news for us. Books are where authors typically explain past research most fully, because they have room to breathe that a journal article's length limits don't allow, so a book's literature review is often the clearest account of a debate we can find. We then search the library catalog to see whether Booth Library has them, or whether we need to request them through I-Share or a library loan from another university. Once we have them, we can read how they characterize the debate and look for other sources they cite that appear useful for our own work.

To recap

Search with terms as closely related to your question as possible, narrow your search by searching within the results, and work to find at least one credible article on your question. Then get away from the search box as quickly as you can and see whether that one article can point you directly to important work on your question. Follow its citations backwards in time, follow its “Cited by” link forward in time, and repeat the trip from each useful work you find. This won't work perfectly every time, but going back and forth between searches and the research itself can vastly speed up how fast you find the debate and the reputable, important works.

Part 3

Check how influential a work is

As a bit of quality control, let's check by another method how influential a work is on the question or debate we are after. We do this by roughly checking how often other authors cite it. Google Scholar is a very general search tool, and though it isn't perfect, it gives quick results on how many other works have cited the one we search for.

Part 4

Travel forward in time

In Part 2 the article's citations took us backwards in time to the works it builds on, and now we take the time machine the other way. This adds one more criterion to our hunt for the debate, which is how current we are on it. Up to now we've paid little attention to when something was written and published. We'd like to update our search for two reasons. First, someone may have provided new and better answers to the question we are asking, and that can be useful to know and draw on as we develop our project. Second, our work will be less likely to interest others if we build it only around debating work done 50 years ago, since our work will look stale and less informed than it should.

Follow the “Cited by” link forward in time

Here is a quick and easy way to follow your question forward in time. Click the “Cited by” link in the Google Scholar results, and it takes you to a new page showing all the works that cite the one you started with. There you can browse for relevant work and check publication dates, or you can use the time menu to limit the results to recent work.

This won't produce instant or perfect results, but it does narrow the results and limit them to more current work that is likely on your topic. Using the “Cited by” link, find three useful works on your question published in the last four years. Each recent work you find has its own literature review, so you can go backwards in time from it as well, and the loop starts again. Go back to the search box only once these loops stop turning up new work, and bring along the better search terms you have learned on the way.

Your record of the debate

Once a search turns up one good work, leave the search box and let the loops backwards and forwards in time do most of the work. Record what you find below. Wrap-up

What we learned

  • Finding the debate takes three tasks, which are finding prior work, filtering out what is most relevant and important, and understanding the competing arguments.
  • We turn a question into concrete search terms and use databases as a quick first step toward one or two solid articles.
  • Finding the debate is a loop, so we leave the search box quickly, travel backwards and forwards in time from each good work, and return to searching only when the loops run dry.
  • A credible work's literature review takes us backwards in time to the important work on a question, because experienced authors cite the research that matters.
  • Citation counts, read against how long a work has been out, give a rough sense of how influential it is.
  • Following “Cited by” forward in time keeps the debate current, so our work doesn't rest only on old answers.
