Confidence intervals.

Part 1 · Jelly beans

Start with the jelly bean bowl

This is the bowl from Module 6, which holds 150 beans coded 1 to 6. Its true mean is 3.43, but pretend you can't see it. All you get is one handful. From that handful you build a 95% interval, which is the handful's mean ± 1.96 × SE, where SE is the handful's SD ÷ √n. Does the interval catch the bowl's mean?

Small handfuls give wide intervals, because with only a few beans the mean could land almost anywhere, so the interval has to be wide to have a chance. Large handfuls give narrow intervals. That's the same contrast as the piles in Module 6, now turned into estimates. The next section does it with 3,112 U.S. counties.

    Why fewer than 95%?
    
        
    A margin of error only accounts for random sampling error. It says nothing about systematic error that pushes every poll the same way: people who decline to answer, likely-voter screens, weighting choices, late deciders. When the whole cloud of estimates sits to one side of the truth, adding more polls does not help.

    Check yourself
    
      
    
        You move from 95% to 99% confidence with the same data. Does the interval get wider or narrower?
        
    It gets wider. To capture μ more often, the method has to cast a wider net, so the critical value rises from about 1.96 to about 2.58. Higher confidence costs precision, and only a bigger n buys both.
    
      
    
      
    
        Your interval for 2026 turnout is 45.6 to 53.1. Is there a 95% probability that μ is in that range?
        
    No. μ is a fixed number (49.31), so this particular interval either contains it or doesn't. The 95% is a property of the procedure, meaning that across many samples, 95% of intervals built this way capture μ.
    
      
    
      
    
        Why t and not z? (We use z in class, but here's when t matters.)
        
    SE = SD / √n uses the sample's SD, which is itself an estimate, so there's a little extra uncertainty. The t distribution has fatter tails to cover it, so its critical value is a bit bigger and the interval a bit wider. That matters most in small samples (n = 10: t = 2.26 vs. z = 1.96). Past about 30 the two are close (t = 2.05 at n = 30), and in the hundreds they're nearly identical (t = 1.98 at n = 100), which is why z works well for survey-sized data. Many researchers use t by default for that reason. Try the t/z switch at n = 5.
    
      
    
      
    
        A poll of 800 voters puts Clinton at 47 and Trump at 44, and it reports a margin of error of ±3.5. Is the 3-point lead outside the margin of error?
        
    No. The ±3.5 applies to each candidate's share. The lead is a difference of two shares from the same sample, and its margin of error is roughly double, about ±6.6 points here. Reporters often get this wrong.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    Wrap-up
    What we learned
    
  
    
    
      
      
    • A confidence interval is a range of plausible values for the population value, built as our estimate plus or minus a margin of error.
      • 
      
    • 95% confidence describes the method rather than any one interval. If we drew many samples, about 95% of their intervals would capture the true value, and about 5% would miss it.
      • 
      
    • Intervals get wider with a higher confidence level or a more spread-out variable, and narrower with a larger sample.

    Formulas
    
  
    
    
    Confidence interval for a mean (t)
    
    x‾±tn−1*·SDn
    
    SD is the sample's standard deviation. t* comes from the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, so for 95% and n = 25, t*(24) = 2.064.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Large-sample (z) interval
    
    x‾±z*·SDn
    
    The z* values are 1.282, 1.645, 1.960 and 2.576 for 80, 90, 95 and 99%. They work well when n is large but give intervals that are too narrow when n is small.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Interval for a proportion (Wald)
    
    p^±z*p^(1−p^)n
    
    This interval breaks down when n·p̂ is small, since if p̂ = 0 the interval has zero width. A rule of thumb is to have at least 10 successes and 10 failures.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Margin of error
    
    MOE=z*·SE≈1n(95%, shares near 50%)
    
    The margin of error is half the width of the interval. A poll of 1,000 has a margin of error of about ±3.1 points on each share.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Interval for a lead within one poll
    
    (p^C−p^T)±z*p^C+p^T−(p^C−p^T)2n
    
    The two shares come from the same respondents and move in opposite directions, so the lead's margin of error is close to twice the margin on one share.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    What the confidence level means
    
    
    Before the sample is drawn, the probability that the interval will capture μ is the confidence level. After it is drawn, the interval either contains μ or it doesn't, and we just don't know which.
