Choosing cases to compare.

When we can study only a few cases, the cases we choose decide what the comparison can show. This module covers the two designs we use to choose them, most similar systems and most different systems, and then gives you a deck of research questions to practice on.

Part 1

Few cases, many variables

Statistics lets us hold rival explanations constant by measuring them across hundreds or thousands of cases. Many of the questions political scientists care most about involve only a handful of cases, though, such as a few states, a few countries or a few revolutions, and each case can take months of reading, travel and interviews to study well. Arend Lijphart (1971) called this the problem of many variables and a small number of cases.

Two states compared without a design

Here are two of the contiguous states with their turnout in the 2024 presidential election (ballots counted as a share of eligible voters) as the outcome. The six rival explanations are the census region, a cold November (a typical November below 35°F), same-day registration, a close 2024 race (a presidential margin under 5 points), a governor race on the 2024 ballot, and the number of cities of 250,000 or more. Each box on the board is one of those rivals for why their turnout differs. An arrow stays live as long as the two states differ on that variable, because a difference in turnout could come from any difference between the states, and an arrow is cut when the states are the same on it.

Rival explanations

Lijphart suggested three ways out of this problem. With two cases and six rivals, most arrows stay live no matter which states you draw, so the comparison cannot tell us which difference mattered. Lijphart's advice was to add cases where we can, combine related variables into fewer ones, and choose cases that are alike on the variables we are not testing, so that those variables drop out of the comparison. The last suggestion is the design of Part 2.

Because each case takes so long to study well, the number of cases is a choice we make on purpose rather than an arbitrary one. The designs on this page use two to five.

Part 2

Most similar systems for testing hypotheses

A most similar systems (MSS) design starts from a hypothesis, so the test variable (the potential cause we suspect) is the starting point. We take two or more cases that are alike in as many ways as we can manage and expect them to have similar outcomes. If their outcomes differ, we focus on what is different between them, and in MSS we choose and pair the cases at the design stage so that the one difference left is the test variable (Janssen Danyi and Janssen Danyi 2024).

Controlling by holding things the same

Controlling means making an independent variable not vary across the cases we study, since a variable that does not vary cannot explain a difference in outcomes. The table is the classic three-case arrangement. X1 and X2 are controls, X3 is the test variable (the treatment), and Y is the outcome, each coded 1 or 0. Case A has the treatment and the outcome, while cases B and C have neither. The controls are not held constant yet. Tap values in the table to flip them, and watch the board as you make each control the same in every case.

A worked example on direct democracy and lobbying

Do direct-democracy opportunities (referendums and citizen initiatives) change how much cause groups lobby on the inside, by meeting officials and legislators directly, rather than on the outside through public campaigns? The test variable is direct-democracy opportunities, and the outcome is the intensity of insider lobbying. Switzerland and Germany are alike on four variables that matter for how groups lobby, so they make a natural MSS pair. Germany's proportional system also elects some members in single-member districts, and we treat the two systems as alike here while noting that difference.

Part 3

Most different systems for building theory

A most different systems (MDS) design starts from an outcome rather than a hypothesis, so we use it to build theory. We take cases that are different in as many ways as we can determine but share a common outcome, and then we search deeper for attributes the cases might have in common. Those shared attributes become plausible explanations for the outcome. We may suspect some similarity going in, but we do not choose the cases already knowing what it is (Janssen Danyi and Janssen Danyi 2024).

A worked example on women in parliament

Why do some countries elect many women to parliament? We start from the outcome, so we look for countries where women held 30% or more of the lower-house seats in 2005, and we use the class World data (World.sav). Argentina, South Africa and Sweden all cleared that mark, and they differ in region, predominant religion, wealth and colonial history about as much as three countries can. Once the cases were chosen, we turned over four further attributes to see whether any of them is shared. The gender quota row comes from International IDEA's Gender Quotas Database rather than the World data.

The two explanations still standing are a PR system and gender quotas.

shared, so still standingdiffers, so cut

In this design the colors flip, so every row that differs turns green. Region, religion, wealth and colonial ruler all differ across the three countries, so none of them can explain an outcome the three share, and their arrows are cut. The same goes for the timing of women's suffrage and the regime type. Two arrows survive. All three countries elect their lower house by proportional representation, and all three also used gender quotas of some kind around 2005, Argentina by law since 1991 and South Africa's ANC and Sweden's major parties through voluntary party quotas.

The design cannot tell these two apart, and they often travel together, since a quota is easiest to apply to a party list. This is the main limit of MDS. An attribute we never check can never be cut, so if we had left quotas off the strip, PR would have looked like the only explanation still standing. Tracing would look at how parties in each country drew up their candidate lists, and an MSS design could compare PR countries with and without quotas.

The suffrage row also holds a small measurement lesson. The World data date South African women's suffrage to 1930, but that law covered white women only, and all South African women could vote only from 1994. The dates differ across the three countries either way, so the design does not change, but it is a reminder to check what a coded value actually measures.

Part 4

Two causal logics in case studies

You saw the outcome the whole time you chose cases, because a case study does not stop at the comparison. Case studies lean mostly on careful evidence of how something happened, and they combine two causal logics to get there.

Controlling

The design does this work. The cases we choose hold rival explanations constant (in MSS) or let them drop away because they differ (in MDS), so fewer arrows are left standing.

Tracing

Inside each case, we gather evidence on how the outcome came about by following the story one step at a time. We try to close the gaps between steps and to back each step with more than one source.

Controlling by design is the qualitative twin of holding a variable constant in multiple regression, where we add the variable to the equation rather than choosing cases that share it. The arrows on the boards are the same X, Y and Z diagrams that appear in Problems of causation, and Part 5 takes the tracing side further.

Part 5

Tracing the steps inside a case

Controlling by design tells us which rival explanations are left standing, but it says little about how X actually produced Y. Process tracing looks inside a single case for the chain of steps that carried X to Y, which George and Bennett (2005) call the intervening causal process. Every step we can document breaks one long inferential leap into shorter ones, and every piece of evidence we place in a gap shrinks the leap that is left.

Shrink the leap

Our case is France before the Revolution, as Skocpol (1979) traced it. The potential cause (X) is the debt the French crown ran up fighting Britain from 1778 to 1783, and the outcome (Y) is the social revolution that began in the summer of 1789. With nothing in between, a reader has to leap the whole way from X to Y on faith. Tap “Add a step” to place the steps of Skocpol’s story, and then tap each dashed link to put its evidence in the gap.

Hoop tests and smoking guns

Not every piece of evidence in a gap does the same work. Van Evera (1997) and Bennett (2010) sort evidence by what it can do to an explanation, and two of their tests matter most for us.

Hoop test

This is evidence that must be there if the explanation is right. If it is missing, the explanation fails, but finding it only keeps the explanation alive.

Smoking gun

This is evidence that would be hard to explain if the explanation were wrong. Finding it strongly supports the explanation, but missing it does not rule the explanation out.

Wrap-up

What we learned

  • With only a few cases and many possible causes, we cannot hold rivals constant statistically, so we hold them constant by how we choose the cases.
  • A most similar systems design tests a hypothesis by pairing cases that are alike in as many ways as possible but differ on the test variable and the outcome.
  • A most different systems design builds theory by taking cases that differ in as many ways as possible but share an outcome, then searching for what they have in common.
  • Case studies combine control by design with tracing how the outcome came about inside each case, one step at a time and with more than one source for each step.
