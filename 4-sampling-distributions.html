Sampling distributions

We rarely see a whole population. We see one sample and compute its mean. Draw thousands of samples from real county election returns and watch where the sample means land: they center on the truth, and they cluster tighter as samples grow.

Start with a bowl of jelly beans

This bowl holds 150 jelly beans in six flavors. Each flavor stands for a number from 1 (Coconut) to 6 (Licorice), so the bowl is a population with its own mean and SD. A handful is a sample. Throughout this section you'll compare a small handful with a large one, grabbed from the same bowl.

The small handfuls make a wide, flat pile: a small handful has to land far from the bowl's mean before it counts as weird. The large handfuls make a narrow pile, and even a modest miss is surprising. Both piles take on the shape of the normal curve, the large one more closely. The spread of each pile is its standard error. The next section does the same thing with a real population: 3,112 U.S. counties.

Check yourself

      

        At n = 25 the standard error of Biden share is about 3.2 points. What will it be at n = 100?
        
About 1.6 points. The standard error is σ/√n, so quadrupling n divides it by √4 = 2. Precision grows with the square root of the sample size, which is why polls with 4,000 respondents are not four times better than polls with 1,000.

      

      

        The population of county vote shares is right-skewed. What shape is the sampling distribution at n = 50?
        
Close to normal. The Central Limit Theorem says the distribution of sample means approaches a normal curve as n grows, whatever the population's shape. Its center is still μ.

      

      

        Why does Total votes cast need a much larger n before the bell shape appears?
        
A few enormous counties sit far out in the right tail. A small sample that happens to include one of them gets a huge mean. The more extreme the skew, the larger n has to be before the Central Limit Theorem takes hold.

      

      

        Biden won about 51% of the national vote. Why is μ only about 33%?
        
The unit of analysis is the county, and every county counts once. Loving County, Texas (66 votes) weighs as much as Los Angeles County. Most counties are small, rural and Republican. Always ask what one row of the data represents.

Formulas

    
  

  

    
Population mean and standard deviation

    μ=1N∑i=1Nxiσ=1N∑i=1N(xi−μ)2
    
Computed once from all N = 3,112 counties. In real research these are the unknowns.

  

  

    
Sample mean

    x‾=1n∑i=1nxi
    
A statistic: it changes from sample to sample. Panel C collects those changing values.

  

  

    
Center of the sampling distribution

    E(x‾)=μ
    
The sample mean is unbiased. On average it hits the population mean exactly.

  

  

    
Standard error of the mean

    SE(x‾)=σn
    
The standard deviation of the sampling distribution. Quadruple n and the SE halves. In practice σ is unknown and we plug in the sample SD, s.

  

  

    
Central Limit Theorem

    x‾∼approx.N(μ,σn)
    
For large enough n the sample mean is approximately normal, whatever the shape of the population. Heavier skew needs a larger n.

  

  

    
Proportions (a 0/1 variable)

    p^=x‾SE(p^)=p(1−p)n
    
For a 1/0 variable the mean is the share of 1s, and σ = √(p(1−p)).

  

  

    
Finite population correction

    SE=σnN−nN−1
    
Sampling without replacement from a finite population shrinks the SE slightly. With N = 3,112 and n ≤ 100 the factor is at least 0.984, so this page ignores it.
