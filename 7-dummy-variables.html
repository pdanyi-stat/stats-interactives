Dummy variables

Regression needs numbers, but region, party and gender are categories. A dummy variable turns a category into a 0/1 switch. Its coefficient is a difference between groups, and in a regression with other IVs it shifts the line up or down.

Part 1 · One dummy

A dummy is a 0/1 switch, and its slope is a difference in means

Code South = 1 and every other state = 0, then run an ordinary regression. With only two possible x values, the least-squares line passes through the two group means. The constant is the mean of the 0 group, the slope is the gap between the groups, and the run is always exactly 1.

Same test, two commands

Highlighted numbers match. A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test from Module 3.


        

      

    

  


  
  

    
Part 2 · Dummy plus a continuous IV

    
A dummy shifts the line up or down: parallel lines

    
Add metro share as a second IV. Plug in south = 0 and you get the non-South line; plug in south = 1 and the same line moves up or down by the South coefficient. Same slope, different intercepts. The model assumes the regional gap is the same at every metro share, which is why the two lines are parallel.

  

  

  
  

    
Part 3 · More than two categories

    
Four regions, three dummies, one reference group

    
A variable with k categories gets k − 1 dummies. The category left out is the reference: the constant is its mean, and every coefficient is a gap from it. Change the reference and the coefficients change, but the predictions don't.

Check yourself

      

        The constant in ŷ = 4.32·South + 47.56 is 47.56. What is it?
        
The predicted Trump share when South = 0: the mean for non-Southern states. With a dummy-only model the constant is always the reference group's mean.

      

      

        In the parallel-lines model, the South coefficient is about 5.6. Interpret it.
        
Holding metro share constant, Southern states are predicted to give Trump about 5.6 more points than non-Southern states. Because the lines are parallel, that gap is the same at every metro share.

      

      

        The South coefficient is 4.3 in Part 1 but 5.6 in Part 2. Why?
        
Part 2 holds metro share constant. Southern states are a bit more metropolitan on average (by about 3 points with D.C., 2 without), and metro share pulls Trump's share down. Leaving metro share out let that hide part of the regional gap. Bivariate gap = gap holding metro constant + (metro's slope × the difference in metro share).

      

      

        Why not include a dummy for every region?
        
Every state is in exactly one region, so the four dummies always add up to 1. That is identical to the constant, so the model can't separate them (perfect collinearity, the “dummy variable trap”). Leave one category out as the reference.

      

      

        You change the reference from Northeast to South. What changes and what doesn't?
        
The constant and every coefficient change, because they are now gaps from the South. Predicted values, R², and the overall F test do not change: it is the same model described from a different starting point.

Formulas

  
One dummy

    y^=β1D+constantconstant=y‾D=0,β1=y‾D=1−y‾D=0
    
Its t test is the equal-variance two-sample t test.

  
Dummy plus a continuous IV (parallel lines)

    y^=β1X+β2D+constant
    
D = 0: ŷ = β₁X + constant. D = 1: ŷ = β₁X + (constant + β₂). Same slope, intercept shifted by β₂. β₂ is the gap between groups holding X constant; the model assumes that gap is the same at every value of X.

  
k categories

    y^=β1D1+⋯+βk−1Dk−1+constant
    
constant = mean of the reference category; βj = mean of category j − reference mean. In Stata, i.region builds the dummies and ib2.region makes level 2 the reference.
