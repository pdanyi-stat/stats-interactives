● Module 4 · Describing data

Measures of dispersion.

How spread out are the observations? The variance and the standard deviation (SD) measure the typical distance of the observations from the mean. The examples use the Massachusetts counties and the Titanic passenger list.

Part 1

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean

For each observation, find its distance from the mean and square it. Squaring keeps the distances below the mean from canceling out the ones above it. Add the squares, then divide by n − 1.

Lunatics per county, Massachusetts, 1854 (NBR)

s² = Σ(X − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1). The mean is 180.85 and n = 13.

Part 3

What the SD tells you about where observations fall

If a variable is roughly normal, about 68% of observations fall within one SD of the mean and about 95% within two. The SD is in the same units as the variable, which is what makes this useful.

Part 4

Compare two variables on the same scale

You can compare the SDs of two variables directly only when they are measured on the same scale, so that a one-point gap means the same thing on both. Here we take two questions from one survey that use the same answers and the same codes.

Pew Research Center, 2014 post-election survey (2014ElectionSurvey_F26.sav)

Right after the 2014 midterm election, the Pew Research Center interviewed 1,353 American adults by telephone between November 6 and 9. People who said they had voted were asked about their own ballot:

q36. How confident are you that your vote was accurately counted?

Everyone was then asked about the election as a whole:

q37. How confident are you that the votes across the country were accurately counted?

q36 and q37 are coded the same way.

The scale runs from most confident (1) to least confident (4), so a low code means more confidence and a higher mean means less trust in the count. Because both questions share these codes, their means and SDs can be compared directly. q36 has 834 valid answers because only voters were asked, while q37 has 1,309.

The question we want to answer is which belief varies more from person to person, confidence in your own vote or confidence in the national count.

In SPSS, go to Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Frequencies, move q36 and q37 over, check Bar charts under Charts, and under Statistics check Mean, Std. deviation, Variance, Skewness and Kurtosis.

Part 6

Settle the Thanksgiving argument

Every Thanksgiving, two of your relatives have the same argument about where the two parties are headed and what the people in each party want from their leaders. You have survey data that can settle it. You'll settle it once with data from 2014 and then again with data from 2024, which came ten years later.

The argument

Aunt Sue thinks a small, very conservative faction has taken over the Republican Party, and that most Democrats are moderate. Uncle Jim thinks ordinary Republicans in general want the party to be more conservative, and that Democrats are pushing their party far to the left. Between them they make four claims, and two are about each party.

Aunt Sue on Republicans (claim 1)People who make up the Republican party have been overrun by some small faction of people who are super conservative and want to push leaders in Congress to be so much more conservative than “old fashioned real Republicans” used to be.
Uncle Jim on Republicans (claim 2)Republicans in general want more conservative policies from leadership. It isn't some small group, but rather the conservative voter today in general wants a far more conservative leadership in the party.
Uncle Jim on Democrats (claim 3)Democrats in America today are so super liberal that they are pushing their leaders in Congress to adopt some communist far left policies.
Aunt Sue on Democrats (claim 4)The Democrats I know are actually pretty moderate, and if anything I think Democrats in general want moderate left policies from Democratic leaders in Congress and DC.

What would settle it? Sue and Jim agree that some Republicans want a more conservative party. They disagree about how many. So the key number is the share who want their party to move. A small share supports Sue, and a large share or a majority supports Jim. The mode and the median tell you where most party members are, and the skew tells you which way the distribution leans and whether the people who want change are a tail or the bulk.

Round 2

Thanksgiving 2024, ten years later

A decade on, Sue and Jim are making the same four claims word for word. A lot has changed in both parties since 2014, so check whether the 2014 answers still hold. You have two sources this time, and they measure different things.
