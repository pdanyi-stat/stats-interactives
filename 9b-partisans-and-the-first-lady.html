Partisans and the First Lady.

How to make these graphs in SPSS
  1. Graphs → Legacy Dialogs → Scatter/Dot → Simple Scatter. Put the IV (dem_therm or rep_therm) on the X axis and the DV (laura_bush_therm) on the Y axis. Click OK.
  2. Double-click the plot to open the Chart Editor. Click Bin Element, and under Count Indicator choose Color Intensity. With many observations, color works better than marker size.
  3. Click Add a reference line from Equation and type the regression line, e.g. y = −0.393x + 89.177. Click Apply. Double-click the line to change its color, weight or style.
  4. Use Add reference line to X Axis and to Y Axis to mark a value of the IV (such as the mean) and its predicted value on the DV. The two lines cross on the regression line.

Reading p for a slope. H₀ says the slope is 0, and p is how often a slope at least this far from 0 would turn up by chance if H₀ were true. So p = .03 means about 3 times in 100, and Stata's 0.000 (SPSS prints .000) means p < .001, or fewer than 1 time in 1,000. If p < .05 we reject H₀, and otherwise we fail to reject it. The .05 cutoff is a convention we choose, and it sets our risk of a Type I error (a false positive). Reading p-values → · Type I and II errors →

Check yourself

Why does the raw scatterplot mislead?

Thermometer answers bunch at round numbers (50, 60, 70, 85, 100), so hundreds of respondents sit on the same few spots. One dot can hide 50 people, and the busiest areas look no busier than a lone outlier. Binning with color intensity shows where the respondents actually are.

Interpret the slope for the Democratic Party thermometer.

Each 1-point warmer rating of the Democratic Party is associated with a predicted Laura Bush rating about 0.39 points lower.

Why compare the average partisan with the most extreme one, rather than just reporting the slope?

A 1-point change on a 0–100 thermometer is hard to picture. Average versus 100 is a comparison readers can relate to, and it turns the slope into a concrete gap on the DV's own scale.

Can we compare these two slopes directly?

Here, yes, roughly, since both IVs are on the same 0–100 scale and the DV is the same. Note that steepness is the size of the effect, while how tightly the cases cluster around the line is r². In this case both point the same way, because the Republican slope is steeper and its r² is higher.

Notes on the data

American National Election Studies, 2004 (classroom extract, 1,212 respondents). Respondents who didn't recognize a figure, couldn't rate it, or refused are left out, so each regression uses about 1,145 people (about 1,170 for Hillary Clinton). Predicted values are computed by hand from the rounded coefficients shown, the way you would on the lab.
