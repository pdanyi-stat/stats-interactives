Crosstabs.

A crosstab sorts cases by two variables at once and counts every group. Here you will watch a crowd turn into a table, read its cells, compare groups with column percentages, and see why an interval variable breaks the table.

Part 1 · From a crowd to a table

Sort the passengers two ways, then count

A crosstab is a way to summarize a group. It counts how many cases fall into each combination of two variables, so it works best when each variable has only a few categories, which usually means nominal or ordinal variables. Here are the 1,309 passengers on the Titanic, sorted by sex and by whether they survived. Step through it, and from step 3 on, tap any cell.

Part 2 · IV across, DV down

Which way we percentage the table

In political science we put the IV across the top, in the columns, and the DV down the side, in the rows. The convention matters because it decides which way we percentage the table, and each way answers a different question.

Part 3 · Compare the columns

Column percentages show an association

The columns of a crosstab usually hold different numbers of cases, so comparing counts can mislead. Column percentages put every group on the same footing, out of 100. If the percentages differ from one column to the next, the two variables are associated. Here are three policy questions from the 2024 American National Election Studies, each split by gender. Part 4 · When a variable is interval

Age in years breaks the table

A crosstab needs a few categories. Once one variable is interval, with many possible values, the table splits into dozens of thin columns, and many of them hold too few people to compare. Here is survival by age on the Titanic, for the 1,046 passengers whose age is known. Try each way of grouping age.

Check yourself

Why do we put the IV in the columns?

It's the convention in political science, and it tells us to percentage within columns. Column percentages compare the groups of the IV with each other, which is what a hypothesis about the IV asks.

339 women and 161 men survived. Were women about twice as likely to survive?

No. The counts ignore how many women and men were aboard. Of the 466 women, 73% survived, and of the 843 men, 19% survived, so women were nearly four times as likely to survive.

65% of women and 53% of men want the minimum wage raised. Is there an association?

Yes. The column percentages differ by about 12 points, so knowing someone's gender tells us something about their answer. Module 5 shows how to measure how strong an association like this is.

When does a crosstab stop being the right tool?

When one of the variables is interval and takes many values. The columns get too thin to compare, and grouping them throws away detail, so we compare means or fit a regression instead.
