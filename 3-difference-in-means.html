    Key idea
    
Reading a p-value

    
Every test asks the same question: if H₀ were true and nothing were really going on, how often would chance alone give us a difference this large or larger? The p-value is that “how often.”

    

    

      
p = .05move the decimal 2 places5 times in 100

      
p = .01move the decimal 2 places1 time in 100

      
.000SPSS (Stata: 0.000) means p < .001: move 3 placesfewer than 1 in 1,000

    

    
    
      
  • Match the wording to the sign. p = .05 is about 5 times in 100; p < .05 is fewer than 5 times in 100.
    • 
      
  • Two decisions, about H₀ only. p < α: reject H₀. p ≥ α: fail to reject H₀. We never “accept” anything: failing to reject means the evidence wasn't strong enough, not that the groups are the same.
    • 
      
  • p is not the chance that H₀ is true. It's the chance of a result like ours if H₀ were true.
    • 
      
  • .05 is a convention we choose (α), not a law of nature. Set α to .01 above and the bar gets higher.
    • 
      
  • Same logic in regression. There H₀ says the slope is 0, and p is how often a slope at least this steep would turn up by chance.
    •

Check yourself

      

        The two groups' 95% CIs overlap a little. Can the difference still be significant?
        
Yes. The standard error of a difference is √(SE₁² + SE₂²), which is smaller than SE₁ + SE₂. So two intervals can overlap while the interval for the difference excludes zero. If the intervals do not overlap at all, the difference is significant; if they overlap, test the difference directly.

      

      

        H₀ is true. At α = .05, how often will the test reject?
        
About 5% of the time. That is what α means: the false-positive (Type I error) rate you choose in advance. Turn on “Make H₀ true” to check.

      

      

        The true gap is the same at n = 25 and n = 250. Why does the test reject far more often at 250?
        
The standard error shrinks with √n, so the distribution of differences tightens around the true gap and pulls away from zero. The share of samples that reject is the test's power.

      

      

        p = .03. Is there a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true?
        
No. p is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if H₀ were true. It is a statement about the data given H₀, not about H₀ given the data.

      

      

        When should you drop “Assume equal variances”?
        
When the groups' standard deviations differ noticeably, as with Euroscepticism (Conservatives 2.6, Labour 3.3). The Welch version, Stata's unequal option, is the safer default. With equal group sizes the two versions give nearly identical answers.

      

    

  


  

    Data: British Election Panel Study 1997–2001 (BEPS, 1,525 respondents) and the 1988 Chilean plebiscite survey by FLACSO (2,700 respondents), both from the carData R package (Fox & Weisberg), via Rdatasets.
    Each full survey is treated as the population; samples are drawn from it without replacement. Respondents with missing values on the outcome are dropped.
    “Make H₀ true” pools both groups and draws each sample from the pool, so the true difference is exactly zero.

Formulas

  

    
The estimate and the null hypothesis

    x‾1−x‾2H0:μ1−μ2=0
  

  

    
Standard error of the difference (unequal variances)

    SE=s12n1+s22n2=SE12+SE22
    
Always smaller than SE₁ + SE₂, which is why overlapping CIs can hide a significant difference.

  

  

    
Pooled version (equal variances)

    sp2=(n1−1)s12+(n2−1)s22n1+n2−2SE=sp1n1+1n2
    
Degrees of freedom: n₁ + n₂ − 2.

  

  

    
Test statistic and interval

    t=(x‾1−x‾2)−0SE(x‾1−x‾2)±t*·SE
    
Reject H₀ at level α when |t| exceeds the critical value t*, which is exactly when the (1 − α) interval for the difference excludes zero.

  

  

    
Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom

    df=(s12/n1+s22/n2)2(s12/n1)2n1−1+(s22/n2)2n2−1
    
Used when variances are not assumed equal. Usually not a whole number.

  

  

    
Effect size (Cohen's d)

    d=μ1−μ2σ
    
The gap in standard-deviation units. Roughly 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. A tiny d can still be “significant” with a big enough n.
