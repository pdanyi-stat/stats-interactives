● Module 10 · Regression

Multiple regression.

A bivariate slope mixes the effect of X with everything that travels with X. Adding a control variable separates them. On this page you will see what “holding constant” does to the data, why slopes shrink, grow or flip, and how R² and adjusted R² respond as a model grows.

Reading p for a slope. H₀ says the slope is 0, and p is how often a slope at least this far from 0 would turn up by chance if H₀ were true. So p = .03 means about 3 times in 100, and Stata's 0.000 (SPSS prints .000) means p < .001, or fewer than 1 time in 1,000. If p < .05 we reject H₀, and otherwise we fail to reject it. The .05 cutoff is a convention we choose, and it sets our risk of a Type I error (a false positive). Reading p-values → · Type I and II errors →

Check yourself

      

        What does “holding income constant” mean for these 51 states?
        
We compare states as if they had the same median income. Statistically, we use only the variation in X₁ that is not predictable from income, which is what panel A plots. It is not the same as an experiment, since anything left out of the model can still be doing the work.

      

      

        The bivariate slope of white poverty is 2.66, but holding income constant shrinks it to 0.93. What happened?
        
Poorer white populations tend to be in lower-income states, and lower income itself predicts a higher Trump share. Much of white poverty's bivariate slope was really income's effect riding along. Bivariate slope = direct slope + (income's slope × how much income moves with white poverty).

      

      

        When does leaving out X₂ not bias β₁?
        
Leaving out X₂ does not bias β₁ when X₂ doesn't affect Y (β₂ = 0) or when X₂ is unrelated to X₁ (δ = 0). Omitted variable bias needs both, meaning the left-out variable must matter for Y and also travel with X₁.

      

      

        Why can R² rise when you add random noise?
        
Least squares will use any variable to shave off a little error in this particular sample, even when the variable is pure chance. Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − k − 1) subtracts a penalty per IV, so it rises only if the new variable improves the fit by more than chance would.

      

      

        Can you compare coefficients across IVs measured in different units?
        
Not directly, because a 1-point change in unemployment and a $1,000 change in income aren't comparable. Standardized coefficients (Stata's beta option) express each effect per 1-SD change in X, in SDs of Y, which puts them on a common scale.

Formulas

  
The model

    y^=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+βkXk+constant
    
Each β is the predicted change in Y for a 1-unit change in that X, holding every other X in the model constant.

  
Holding constant, step by step (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell)

    β1=slope of eY|X2 on eX1|X2
    
Residualize Y and X₁ on X₂, then regress leftovers on leftovers. That is panel A.

  
Omitted variable bias

    bbivariate=β1+β2·δ
    
δ is the slope from regressing X₂ on X₁, and this holds exactly for least squares in any sample.

  
Adjusted R²

    Radj2=1−(1−R2)n−1n−k−1
    
k is the number of IVs. Each added IV costs a degree of freedom.

  
Standardized coefficient (Stata's beta)

    βj*=βj·SDXjSDY
    
This is the change in Y, in SDs, for a 1-SD change in Xj.

  
Tests

    
For each coefficient, t = βj/SE with n − k − 1 degrees of freedom. For the whole model, F tests whether all slopes are zero at once.
