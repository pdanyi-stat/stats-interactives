Organizing a literature review.

Now that you have located some potentially useful sources, and through that process have begun to locate the debate over your question, the next step is to organize the arguments made in that debate. Crafting a coherent and targeted literature review is to some extent an art form, but there are good principles and guidelines to follow as you develop the skill of writing about what scholars have said about your research question.

[DRAFT ONLY] This page is a draft, and its parts may still change.

Part 1

Why we write literature reviews

We write literature reviews that summarize prior work for two reasons. First, we wish to show that our work fits well within a body of knowledge, which lends it credibility and lets it draw on the accumulated work of others. Second, a literature review shows how our work is an original contribution to the accumulated knowledge of the field, and to our question specifically.

Part 2

Group the arguments by the influences they stress

As you begin to process your sources into a review, try to identify groups of similar arguments about your research question. Grouping arguments pushes us to recognize both what they have in common and what sets them apart from other arguments about the question. Suppose your question is why some people participate in elections and others do not. One source might argue that it has to do with income, and another that it depends on whether a person lives in a wealthy neighborhood. Both are saying that voting is related to social and economic status, so we can group them together. Two other sources might argue that voting is explained by childhood experiences with parents or relatives who vote, and we can group those together as family influences, or what we refer to as socialization.

Name each influence in general terms

One way to build these groups is to find the important influence in each argument and make sure it is expressed in general terms.

Influence in the sourceGeneral term
IncomeEconomic resources
FriendsSocialization
ChurchesBelief systems or values
CommunityNetworks, or “social capital”

You can generate some of these terms yourself, and you will find others in your sources. If the authors writing on your question use certain general terms, it is often useful to adopt them.

Part 3

Group them by their evidence and cases

A second way to group your sources is by the types of evidence they use, or by the cases they draw on, meaning the examples, countries, timeframes, places and people. For example, some works on your question may reach one conclusion based on evidence from the United States only. That is a good observation for a literature review, and it may also be a weakness.

Part 4

Weigh the strengths and weaknesses

Finally, a good literature review identifies the strengths and weaknesses of the arguments made by the various groups, or “camps,” on your question. A critique can rest on faulty logic, poor use of evidence, contrary evidence, a limited timeframe for cases, a focus on only one or a few places, too much focus on one region or ignoring other important influences, among a myriad of other types of critique. Practicing informed critique matters for your own research and review, and it also sharpens how you read research in the future, so that you move from reading to critical reading. And as we identify weaknesses in prior explanations, we make the case that there is room, and a rationale, for our own project.

A model from the course paper template

These two paragraphs come from the literature review in the course research paper template, on why states condemn other states that violate human rights. The highlighted passages carry the margin notes from the template, so tap one to read it. Notice how the first paragraph groups several works into one camp and the second names exactly what that camp can't explain.

Much of the research on human rights and transnational networks argues that third party state condemnations – or other forms of pressure – are a political outcome of the lobbying efforts of activist networks including NGOs and INGOs (Keck and Sikkink 1998; Risse and Sikkink 1999; Murdie and Davis 2012).1 Domestic activists facing repression “link up with transnational networks and INGOs who then convince… great powers to pressure norm violating states” (Risse and Sikkink 1999, 18).2 By “[searching] out international allies to… bring back pressure on their states from outside” (Keck and Sikkink 1998, 12) activists are able to amplify their voices and exert greater political influence than they could alone. Activist networks help gain access to media outlets, sympathetic politicians and bureaucrats, and influential individuals, and use these connections to push target governments to place pressure on norm-violating states.

In many cases of human rights violations, large numbers of third-party states do not face substantial pressure from activist networks or human rights NGOs, yet many of these states still pressure violators to halt. Theories of transnational activism hold that activists and NGOs are strategic in their selection of what constitutes “target actors” (Keck and Sikkink 1998, 23) in a given campaign. While these theories of transnational activism are correct in that condemning and sanctioning behavior is often the outcome of direct and selective activist pressure, they offer less insight into why many, even a majority, of states act to condemn or sanction violators who face no pressure.3

Part 5

Organize your own sources

Now begin organizing the sources you have found on your own question. Research questions develop and shift, so start by restating yours in its current form. In each group, give it a label, provide the citations for the works, and say why you grouped them together.

Groups by evidence, cases or timeframes

If there are no clear categories, that's fine, so just detail these elements for each source.

Strengths and weaknesses, source by source

First list a few for each source, briefly.

Two paragraphs of critique

Now discuss these weaknesses and strengths without simply listing them, which means talking about the arguments in relation to each other across your sources. Some sources are more important and deserve more attention, and that is perfectly normal. Aim for no more than two paragraphs, and focus on getting to the essence of the strengths and weaknesses with precise writing, since critique is best when it is carefully crafted.

Wrap-up

What we learned

  • A literature review shows that our work fits a body of knowledge and shows where it makes an original contribution.
  • We group arguments by the influence they stress, named in general terms such as economic resources, socialization or social capital.
  • We also group works by the evidence and cases they use, which can reveal a shared weakness, such as relying on one country.
  • Critique across the groups, rather than source by source, is what opens space for our own project.
