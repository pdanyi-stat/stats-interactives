Visualizing data.

A chart lets a reader see a pattern before reading a single number, so it is often the first thing people look at in our work and sometimes the only thing they remember. This module walks through charts built in SPSS. The first path covers which kind of chart fits which kind of variable and test, and the second covers how to edit a chart down to its data and how to build panels that compare groups side by side.

Before we start

Three jobs for a chart

Most charts in research do one of three jobs. Some show counts across cases, such as how many patients each county reported. Some show the attributes of one variable, such as where its values bunch up and how far they spread. The rest show a relationship between two variables, which is the job behind every technique from crosstabs to regression. Which chart fits depends on the job and on the level of measurement of each variable, so that is where Path A starts. Path B then takes the charts SPSS gives us and edits them, because the defaults spend more ink than the data need.

Path AChoosing the formWhich chart fits which variable and which technique, built step by step in SPSS. Path BEditing the inkStrip charts down to their data, keep scales honest, and build panels that compare groups.

Every chart on this page is drawn to look like SPSS output in the classic (legacy) chart style, with the gray plot area, the black frame and the khaki bars. SPSS 25 and later use a newer default look, but the dialogs, the Chart Editor and the editing steps are the same. The Stata drawers give the matching commands.

Path A · Part 1

Counts across cases

Our data here are the 13 counties of Massachusetts in 1854, from a state report that counted the people it called “lunatics” in each county (pardon the term, it was the language of the era and the dataset). Each county is one case, and the variable Lunatics Per County holds its count, so we want one bar per case.

One bar per county

In SPSS, go to Graphs → Legacy Dialogs → Bar, choose Simple and Values of individual cases, since our cases are counties and we want the count within each one. Put Lunatics Per County in Bars Represent and choose Variable: County for the category labels.

Path B · Part 4

Strip the ink

Edward Tufte (2001) asks how much of the ink on a chart shows data, which he calls the data-ink ratio, and his advice is to erase ink that shows nothing, within reason. SPSS defaults use more ink than the data need, so a reader has to look past a gray background, a frame and heavy fills to find the counts. Start from the sorted bar chart in Part 1, and tap each edit to make it. The two meters track how much ink is left that shows no data and how much ink the whole chart uses, and each one compares the chart with the SPSS default.

From the SPSS default to a clean chart

Double-click the chart in the Output Viewer to open the Chart Editor. Most edits work the same way, since you double-click the element you want to change, then change it on the tab that opens.

Path B · Part 4, continued

Four more charts to strip

The same habits apply to every chart SPSS makes, including scatterplots, box plots and error bar charts. Work through each one, tapping the numbered edits in order, and notice that some edits add ink, such as a fit line, case labels or a printed mean, because that ink carries data the default leaves out.

Path B · Part 5

Honest scales

Erasing ink should never change what the data say. Tufte's lie factor measures how far a chart distorts them, as the size of an effect shown in the graphic divided by the size of the effect in the data. A lie factor of 1 is honest, and anything far from 1 overstates or understates the difference. Alberto Cairo (2019) shows how often charts in the news break this rule without anyone meaning to.

Path B · Part 6

Panels for side-by-side comparison

A panel chart draws the same chart once for each group of a third variable and lines the copies up on shared axes. In SPSS, every legacy dialog has a Panel by box with two slots, Rows and Columns. Columns are usually the better choice when we compare groups, as with box plots or error bars for a test, because the panels then share one Y axis and the eye compares them straight across. Wrap-up

What we learned

  • A chart has a job, whether that is showing counts across cases, the attributes of one variable or a relationship, and the job together with the level of measurement decides which chart fits.
  • Each test has a matching chart, such as percentage bars for a crosstab, means with error bars for a t test and a scatterplot with a line for regression, and the chart often shows the result before the output does.
  • Software defaults spend ink on backgrounds, frames and fills, so we erase what shows no data and keep everything that does.
  • A scale has to show effects at their true size, and the lie factor measures how far a chart drifts from that.
  • Panels repeat one chart for each group on shared axes, and panels in columns put the groups side by side on one Y axis, which is the easiest way to compare them.
