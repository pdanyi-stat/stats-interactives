● Module 5 · How much does knowing X help?

Proportional reduction in error.

Lambda, gamma and r² look like three unrelated statistics, but each one answers the same question. Once you know the independent variable (X), how many fewer mistakes do you make when you guess the dependent variable (Y)? We will play that guessing game three times, once for each level of measurement.

E₁
Errors without X

First we guess every case using only what we know about Y itself. Depending on the level of measurement, that guess is Y's mode, a coin flip, or Y's mean.

E₂
Errors with X

Then we guess each case again, and this time we use what we know about its X. That means the mode within its column, the order of the cases on X, or the regression line.

PRE
(E₁ − E₂) / E₁

This is the share of our errors that knowing X eliminates. A value of 0 means X does not help at all, and a value of 1 means knowing X leaves no errors.

λ LambdaFor a nominal Y, we guess the most common category. γ GammaFor ordinal X and Y, we guess which of two cases ranks higher. r² R-squaredFor an interval Y, we guess a number and measure each error as a squared distance.

Part 1 · Lambda (nominal)

Guess the vote

Recall that lambda is the measure of correlation we use for nominal variables. Without knowing X, your best strategy is to guess the most common answer (the mode) for everyone. Once you know X, you guess the most common answer (the mode) within each column instead. Lambda tells us what share of the first strategy's errors the second strategy saves. We will start with 20 people so that you can count every guess, and then move up to full survey tables.

Guess the party of 20 voters

These 20 people are ten city people and ten country people, drawn at random from the Democrats and Republicans in the 2024 American National Election Studies (ANES). The survey asks whether people usually think of themselves as a city person, a suburban person, a small-town person or a country person, and we use the two ends of that list. You will guess each person's party twice, and both times you guess the most common party (the mode). The first time you don't know whether anyone is a city or a country person, and the second time you do. Count your wrong guesses each time, since those counts are what lambda compares.

Lambda with real survey tables

The steps are the same with larger tables. In each table at least one variable is nominal (it has no built-in order), such as religious tradition or whether someone is a city or country person, so lambda is still the right measure, and you still guess the most common answer (the mode). The only thing that changes is the size of the tables, which now have more categories and thousands of respondents instead of 20.

  • E₁: wrong guesses when everyone gets the overall mode (the outlined total).
  • E₂: wrong guesses when each column gets its own mode (the blue cells).
  • λ = (E₁ − E₂) / E₁, which is the same formula you used for the 20 voters.

Part 2 · Gamma (ordinal)

    
Do pairs line up?

    
When both variables have ordered categories, we use gamma, and gamma asks a slightly different question. Take two people at random. If one of them ranks higher on X, gamma guesses that the same person also ranks higher on Y. Pairs where that guess is right are called concordant, pairs where it is wrong are called discordant, and pairs that are tied on either variable are set aside.

  


  

    
Who is more conservative?

    
Here are six voters from the 2024 American National Election Studies, placed by party ID (X) and ideology (Y). Since both variables are ordered, gamma does not guess anyone's category. It guesses the order of each pair instead, asking which of the two voters is more conservative.

    

      

      
    

    

      

      

    

  

  

    
Gamma with real survey tables

    
Comparing pairs works the same way with thousands of respondents. Both variables are ordinal, so we again predict the order within each pair and set the tied pairs aside.

    
    
      
  • E₁: half of the untied pairs, the coin flip.
    • 
      
  • E₂: the discordant pairs, where “higher on X, higher on Y” guesses wrong.
    • 
      
  • γ = (E₁ − E₂) / E₁ = (concordant − discordant) / (concordant + discordant). With thousands of respondents there are millions of pairs, so we draw them at random instead of drawing every line. After that, we count every pair exactly by going through the table cell by cell.

Count every pair, one cell at a time

    
Drawing pairs at random only estimates γ. To get it exactly, we walk through the table one cell at a time. The people in each cell pair with everyone below and to the right, who are further along on both X and Y, so those pairs are concordant. They also pair with everyone below and to the left, who are further along on Y but not on X, so those pairs are discordant. People in the same row or column are tied and set aside.

Part 3 · R-squared (interval)

    
Guess the mean, or use the line?

    
When Y is a number, the best single guess we can make without X is the mean, ȳ. Each error is the vertical distance from a point to that guess, and we square those distances and add them up to get E₁. Once we know X, we guess from the line of best fit instead, the one straight line that comes closest to all of the points (also called the regression line). The squared distances to that line add up to E₂. Switch between the two guesses and watch how the error sticks change.

  
Want to know more about the line of best fit? Jump to Module 9, where you can drag the points and watch the line refit.

  


  

    
Guess six ratings of Laura Bush

    
Here are six respondents from the 2004 National Election Study. Y is how warmly each of them rates Laura Bush on a 0 to 100 feeling thermometer, and X is how warmly they rate the Republican Party on the same scale. Since Y is a number, a guess can now be off by a little or by a lot, so we measure each miss as a distance, square it, and add the squares up.

Check yourself

      

        λ = .84 for 2024 presidential vote by party ID. Say it in one sentence.
      
Knowing someone's party ID cuts our errors in predicting their 2024 presidential vote by 84% compared with always guessing the most common vote.

      

      

        Why can λ be 0 when the two variables are clearly related?
        
Lambda only counts changes in the guess. If the same category is the mode in every column, knowing X never changes the prediction, so no errors are saved, even if the column percentages differ. Use a chi-square test or Cramér's V to detect that kind of association.

      

      

        Gamma ignores tied pairs. Why does that matter?
        
With few categories, most pairs are tied and gamma is computed from the rest, so it tends to look stronger than other ordinal measures. Kendall's tau-b counts ties in the denominator and is usually smaller for the same table.

      

      

        r² = .31 for Biden share on county size. Does that mean 31% of counties are predicted correctly?
        
No. It means the regression line removes 31% of the squared prediction error we make by guessing the mean. In other words, county size accounts for 31% of the variation in Biden's vote share across counties, and 69% is left unexplained.

Formulas

  

    
Every PRE measure

    PRE=E1−E2E1
    
E₁: errors predicting Y without X. E₂: errors predicting Y with X.

  

  

    
Lambda (Y in rows, X in columns)

    λ=(N−maxRi)−(N−∑jmaxnij)N−maxRi
    
Ri: row totals. max nij: the largest cell in column j. Lambda is asymmetric, so predicting Y from X can give a different λ than predicting X from Y.

  

  

    
Gamma

    γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd
    
Ns: concordant (same-order) pairs. Nd: discordant pairs. As a PRE measure, guessing the order at random is wrong on half the untied pairs, so E₁ = (Ns + Nd)/2, and following X is wrong on the discordant ones, so E₂ = Nd. Then (E₁ − E₂)/E₁ = γ.

  

  

    
R-squared

    r2=∑(yi−y‾)2−∑(yi−y^i)2∑(yi−y‾)2=1−SSresidualSStotal
    
In Stata's regress output, E₁ is the Total SS and E₂ is the Residual SS. In bivariate regression r² is also the square of Pearson's r.

  

  

    
Which measure when

    
Use lambda when Y is nominal, gamma (or Kendall's tau-b or Somers' d) when X and Y are ordinal, and r² when Y and X are interval. Always say what errors were reduced, whether they are wrong guesses of a category, wrongly ordered pairs, or squared distances.
