● Practice · Putting it all together

Do news deserts vote less?

More than 200 counties in our data have no local news outlet at all, and hundreds more are down to one. Local news tells people who is running and what is on the ballot, so a reasonable hypothesis is that counties without it vote less. This page applies every technique on the site to that question, using 2026 midterm turnout in 2,709 counties, and each step links back to its full module if you need a refresher.

The data

Each row is one county. Our DV, turnout26, is the number of votes cast in the state's top 2026 race divided by the county's citizens of voting age, as a percentage. The main IV, news, counts the county's local news outlets in the Medill State of Local News Project's 2026 report, and a county with none is a news desert. The 2026 votes are unofficial counts as of Nov. 25, 2026, so every number on this page will move a little once states certify.

Step 1 · Modules 2–4: central tendency, skew and dispersion

Describe the counties

A variable's level of measurement decides which average makes sense. For interval-ratio variables, skew decides whether the mean still sits in the middle, and county population is about as skewed as a variable gets.

Step 2 · Module 5: crosstabs, lambda and gamma

Does knowing X help you guess a county's turnout?

For a crosstab, we group turnout26 into low turnout (under 45%), middle turnout (45% to 54.9%) and high turnout (55% or more). The turnout groups (the DV) run down the rows and the IV runs across the columns, and the cells show column percentages. Lambda fits nominal IVs, and gamma fits ordered ones.

Step 4 · Module 7: confidence intervals

Build a confidence interval by hand

The interval is centered on the sample mean. We choose how confident to be, and that choice sets z. The SD and n set the standard error, and everything else is arithmetic.

Step 5 · Module 8: difference in means

Testing the news deserts

Each test assumes H₀ (no difference) and asks how often a gap this large or larger would show up if H₀ were true. We reject H₀ or fail to reject it, and we never accept anything. Some of these tests come out the way the hypothesis predicts, and some do not, so read the direction every time.

Read first

These are counties, not voters

Every number on this page describes counties. A finding that news deserts have higher or lower turnout says nothing direct about whether a person without a local paper is less likely to vote, since the people who do vote in a news desert may be the ones who get their news elsewhere. Reading a county pattern as a fact about individuals is the ecological fallacy. Keep that in mind as Steps 6–8 add controls. Each slope there is a difference between counties in points of turnout.

Step 6 · Module 9: bivariate regression

One IV at a time

We start with a dummy IV (news desert or not) and then a continuous one (the share of adults with a college degree). The equation is the same either way, y = mx + b, or in stats notation f(x) = β₁X₁ + constant.

Step 7 · Module 10: multiple regression

News deserts, holding education and county size constant

On its own, the news-desert slope is small and not significant. Add controls and watch it move, first up and then back down. Every model here uses all 2,709 counties, so the comparison is fair.

Step 8 · Module 11: dummy variables

Size dummies, then size, news and education together

County size has four categories, so it gets three dummies. The category left out is the reference, so the constant is its mean and each coefficient is a gap from it. Then we add desert and college, and each dummy shifts a line up or down, which gives eight parallel lines, one for each combination of size and news desert.

Check yourself

The mean of turnout26 is 49.3. What does that number mean?

In the average county, about half of the citizens of voting age voted in the state's top 2026 race. It is an average of counties, so a county of 500 people counts as much as Los Angeles County.

News deserts vs. counties with local news: Sig. = .234. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. A gap this size would turn up often by chance if news deserts and other counties had the same mean turnout. That isn't evidence that local news doesn't matter, only that these data give no evidence of a difference between the two kinds of county.

The slope on desert is 0.90. Where have you seen that number before?

You saw it in Step 5, where it is the news deserts' mean minus the mean of the counties with local news. A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test, and it gives the same t and p.

The desert slope is +0.9 alone, +3.0 holding college constant and +1.0 holding college and size constant. Why?

News deserts have fewer college graduates than other counties, and education goes with higher turnout, so holding college constant takes away a handicap and the desert slope rises. But most news deserts are tiny counties, and the smallest counties turn out at high rates whether or not they have local news. Once size is held constant too, the desert slope falls back to about 1 point and is no longer significant.

With Under 10,000 as the reference, the coefficient for 10,000–49,999 is −3.6. What does it mean?

On average, counties of 10,000 to 49,999 people turned out about 3.6 points lower than counties under 10,000. Turnout dips in the middle sizes and rises again in the biggest counties, which a single size slope could not show.

Sources and data
Data: 2026 county turnout for 2,709 counties in 38 states (newsdeserts_turnout2026.sav). Local news outlets per county come from the Medill State of Local News Project's 2026 report. 2026 votes are AP county results as published by NBC News, counted for the governor's race where there was one and otherwise the U.S. Senate race, and they are unofficial counts as of Nov. 25, 2026, before states certified. Turnout divides those votes by the citizen voting-age population from the American Community Survey, 2019–2023, which also supplies population, education and median age, and 2024 turnout uses Tony McGovern's county presidential results. The page treats the counties as a sample to practice inference. Step 3 draws counties at random with replacement. Confidence intervals use z, as in class.

Formulas

Confidence interval for a mean

x‾±z×SDn

z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.58 (99%).

Lambda

λ=E1−E2E1

E₁ counts the errors from guessing the DV's mode for everyone, and E₂ counts the errors from guessing each IV column's mode.

Gamma

γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd

Nₛ counts pairs ranked the same way on both variables, and N_d counts pairs ranked in opposite ways. Ties are ignored.

Difference in means (equal variances)

t=x‾1−x‾2SDp1n1+1n2

SDₚ is the pooled SD of the two groups. H₀ says the difference is 0.

Multiple regression

y^=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+constant

Each β is the predicted change in turnout26 for a one-unit change in that X, holding the other IVs constant.
