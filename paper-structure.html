Anatomy of a research paper.

A research paper follows a structure that readers in political science expect, and it runs from the abstract through the works cited. This page walks through each section with the course research paper template, in the template's own words, along with its example passages and the margin notes that explain what each passage is doing. Tap any highlighted passage to read its note.

The sections, in order

The template speaks in the voice of each section, so “I” below is the section describing its own job. The example passages are the template's own. One is about why states condemn human rights violators, and the other comes from Paul Danyi's study of the first municipal airports and shows how cities got them.

Section 1

Title and abstract

The title can be clever or it can be clear and precise, and either way is fine. Under it come your name, the university and the department, and then the abstract.

The abstract, in its own words

I accomplish the following, generally in this order. I state the interesting question I am answering first, and I may even pose it as a puzzle, something we don't know the answer to yet. I then paint a big picture of what research knows on this question in about one sentence. Next I say clearly, in one or two lines, how my work adds something to what we know, if at all possible, or at least that I tried to add to what we know. Then I briefly state the data or cases I draw on and how I go about answering the question. Finally, I clearly state my conclusions based on the research I have done, in one or maybe two sentences.

Write me first as a draft, since I help map the paper out for you, especially if you hate making outlines (and if you like outlines, make one too). Once the paper is done, come back and revise me. Sentences from the paper can often be pasted in here with minor changes, so look for them. And I work in reverse too, since I make a great paragraph at the end of your introduction.

The template's own abstract runs about 230 words, and yours should be roughly that long and no more than 1.5 times that length. The builder at the end of this page lays out the five parts in order.

Section 2

Introduction

The introduction, in its own words

It is my job to take the research and generate some interest on the part of the audience, so I take a step back and drive home how the research relates to problems or issues in the real world. I am your only hope of converting a skeptical audience into willing readers. Creating me is a bit of an art form, so practice a few versions. Write me as a draft, and then, once the paper is nearly done, come back and revise me after you've explained why your research matters to your friends and relatives. If you convinced them it matters, put whatever convinced them here.

Here is an example of generating some interest in a work that otherwise would make most readers glaze over and fall asleep.

How states respond when other states violate human rights standards can be the determining factor in whether the lives of thousands are spared or lost, whether atrocities continue or cease.1 Over the past six decades much progress has been made in improving human rights around the world. Among the most important has been the dismantling and eventual reversal of the “conspiracy of mutual state silence that prevailed until the second half of the twentieth century” (Simmons 2009, 4).2 Prior to this dismantling, states could disregard the violence and repression that other states imposed on their populations with relative impunity, due to strong sovereignty norms including non-interference. At the beginning of the 21st century many states do still remain silent, but doing so places them in a shrinking minority, and remaining silent carries potential political costs both domestic and international. Now, 60 years after the emergence of human rights norms and international institutions, there is an expectation among states that they should lend their diplomatic voices in support of victims of state-led violations of their rights, predominantly when those violations include widespread physical violence. Understanding how this expectation among states came to be and how it remains stable can tell us about the emergence and stability of norms in international society, and more importantly it can warn us of conditions that may lead to the collapse of this expectation, a development that would be a major setback to those struggling to realize their human rights.3

Section 3

Statement of the question and argument in brief

This section, in its own words

I am the brief statement of the research question, and I also provide a condensed version of the argument. It is just fine to lead right off with the question, so don't hide it.

“Why do states decide to condemn or sanction human rights violators in the absence of direct pressure from human rights organizations and activists?”4 Why do states not free-ride and leave norm enforcement behaviors to other states when many face little pressure from human rights NGOs?5

I argue that …6

Section 4

What we know about this topic

The literature review, in its own words

I am the literature review, also known as “what we know.” You need to look at some existing research on the topic, at least four or five recent articles or books, and summarize what others have said here. You don't need to read each whole work, since the key argument it makes is what you're after. There's no need to use me to revolutionize our understanding of the research agenda your question relates to, so keep it simple and clearly tell us what work on your exact question says. Be brief, be clear, be relevant to your question and be explicit about how each work is relevant. Key quotes are where scholars often go overboard, so pick the right quotes and be sure to cite them properly.

What We Know About This Topic7

Much of the research on human rights and transnational networks argues that third party state condemnations – or other forms of pressure – are a political outcome of the lobbying efforts of activist networks including NGOs and INGOs (Keck and Sikkink 1998; Risse and Sikkink 1999; Murdie and Davis 2012).8 Domestic activists facing repression “link up with transnational networks and INGOs who then convince… great powers to pressure norm violating states” (Risse and Sikkink 1999, 18).9 By “[searching] out international allies to… bring back pressure on their states from outside” (Keck and Sikkink 1998, 12) activists are able to amplify their voices and exert greater political influence than they could alone.

In many cases of human rights violations, large numbers of third-party states do not face substantial pressure from activist networks or human rights NGOs, yet many of these states still pressure violators to halt. While these theories of transnational activism are correct in that condemning and sanctioning behavior is often the outcome of direct and selective activist pressure, they offer less insight into why many, even a majority, of states act to condemn or sanction violators who face no pressure.10

Section 5

Empirical, historical or process background

The background, in its own words

I am the selective background about your topic, and I do several useful things. In some papers I give the history of something, but I give only the directly relevant contextual history rather than all of it. So if your question is about student voting, give the reader some context on how student voting has evolved wherever you are studying it. I may also offer contemporary evidence about the question, such as recent turnout and the barriers students face. I help your reader be on the same page as you, and by describing things like barriers to voting I begin to explain your concepts.

I often take the shape of illustrative cases and examples for the more formal analysis later. In the voting-barriers case, you might include an in-depth interview in which a student clearly described how one of the barriers made the difference in voting or not voting. That helps flesh out, and convince the reader of, the mechanisms you will later suggest caused something.

I vary in length, and I typically end up longer than you planned when you started typing me. Keep in mind that you want only what gets your reader on the same page as you, clarifies concepts a bit, gives key context and illustrates mechanisms. I shouldn't read like an untargeted book report that leaves the reader wondering why you are talking about this.

Here is a clip from a paper on how elites mobilized to build a network of airports in the United States in the early 1900s. The paper goes on to use statistical methods to link elites and their organizations to airports getting built, but since that is a big inferential leap, it is best to show that the mechanism is plausible first.

Frequently mobilization for an airport in the community took the form of raising capital. For example, in Milwaukee in 1926 the Association of Commerce air service, an association of businesses and residents, held a meeting where funds were pledged by “the citizens to buy the airport site and later sell it to the city” (The Milwaukee Journal, April 14, 1929).11 Civic groups were so determined to jump on the bandwagon of progress that even when their city, or the federal government, could not offer funds, they were able to raise the funds themselves in order to present the airport to the city or sell it at a reduced price or more opportune moment. In 1930 a small group of property owners in Dallas, Texas, “sensing the importance of commercial aviation” offered the city of Dallas 230 acres for the development of a commercial airport (Airport News Supplement Oct. 1930, 47).12 In Dayton, civic elites went so far as to buy a location north of the city in Vandalia, and named it Dayton Airport even though “… Dayton city officials felt no special relationship to it” (Bednarek 1997, 131).13

Section 6

State your hypotheses and how you'll test them

This section, in its own words

I am the signal that your writing is about to get much easier, so breathe a sigh of relief on arriving here. State your hypotheses very clearly and accurately, and use only the most cursory writing to lead into them. I need no conclusion or transition, so just end me, and people get it.

After all the context above about airports, here is this section from that paper in its entirety.

The above qualitative evidence of city siting processes14 leads me to expect that economic elites and civic groups will have facilitated mobilization for airports systematically across the United States during the 1920s.15 To test the hypothesis that regimes of elites, civic groups, and officials were increasing the likelihood that their city could “get things done” when it came to building an airport, I employ a logistic regression model which explains variations in mobilization for airports within U.S. counties.16 If regime theory is correct, and regimes do facilitate “getting things done” in cities, then I should observe variables associated with elites, civic groups such as business societies, and a general capacity to engage in collective action vary systematically with the successful siting of municipal airports.17

H1: all else equal, cities with the increased presence of urban regimes will be more likely to have successfully built an airport relative to cities with fewer elites.18

Section 7

Cases, data sources, measures and controls

This section, in its own words

I am the section where you explain, in an often boring and straightforward manner, how you decided to measure things, how you generated your key data and how you collected information about potential confounding influences, or controls. I can be medium in length depending on the number of key variables and the research method. Heavily quantitative research generally has shorter, more straightforward sections. In a case-study or qualitative design, you'd discuss case selection here and how it controls for alternative explanations. In a quantitative project, tell the reader how you turned concepts into operationalized variables and then into measures, and where the data come from, for every variable and control. The responsible researcher is transparent and honest here.

The demographic and land data used comes from the 1920 U.S. Census data files. Airport data is taken from a compiled list of “principal” airports as of 1927 in the New York Times.19 There are competing contentions in the historical records as to how many airports there were by the end of the 1920s. For example, MacDonald (1930, 226) indicates as many as 1000 airports in U.S. cities according to the Department of Commerce.20 However, not all airports involved city governments in their construction, and making the political decision and having the ability to build a municipal airport is the question at hand.

I operationalize regimes as the presence of a business community and the elites that operate those businesses. Given that an increase in the business community is likely to correlate highly with business associations and elites, I use a proxy measure for regimes as the number of manufacturing establishments located in the county.21 Education level is measured as the total number of 18-20 year-old individuals that were attending school in 1920.22 I further operationalize23 the capacity to mobilize in a population as the degree of informal connections among them, a precursor to forming urban regimes. I measure this by the proportion of the population that reported attending an identifiably non-minority church in the year 1920.24

Section 8

Model and analysis

This section, in its own words

I am the section in which you present the model for your analysis, or simply the analysis itself, by selecting the relevant statistical, qualitative or case-study evidence to show the reader. In quantitative work that is very straightforward, since it includes many of the measures of association we cover. In qualitative work it is a bit different and likely includes a clear explanation of the important variables across the selected cases, or a discussion of the patterns in something like interview data. Either way, I describe those patterns and associations in clear, sober detail. I am the least readable section, and I can be tedious, but intellectually I should be fairly simple, since the hard work is done by a statistics program or by your careful notes and comparisons.

It is not enough to report coefficients and numbers and blast them at your audience, so offer useful interpretations of them. In a regression, a coefficient of 2.5 means a one-unit increase in the independent variable goes with a 2.5-unit increase in the dependent variable, so pick some meaningful common values of the independent variable and tell the reader how much change in the dependent variable we expect when we move from one to the other. This principle holds for every method we use, and not just for regression. If your interviews showed that students respond differently to barriers that require dealing with local officials than to barriers that require documents, tell the reader what you'd expect if a town changed registration to require that students hand in forms in person to a clerk downtown. That is speculative extrapolation, but such thought experiments illustrate what your data lead us to expect. Be thorough and interpret all your results, and if you have four controls, at least briefly mention whether each one matters.

Section 9

Discussion and conclusion

This section, in its own words

I am the discussion and conclusion, and you can break me into two parts if you want. Either way, I talk about your results, but instead of strictly interpreting them from the data, I comment on what they mean for the bigger picture, the big question and the research you mentioned in the opening sections. Did your conclusions undermine what other authors have said about student voting and voting barriers? Then talk here about how they did, and about what that means for the real world. If you followed good research practice, does your finding mean a state could add barriers to voting without affecting students much, or that such barriers would be inconsistent with students' right to participate in democracy? Now is the time to say so.

Section 10

Works cited

From the template

Add a page break and insert a properly formatted list of citations that includes every work cited, even those in the footnotes. It should be in APSA format. Format matters, and not in a petty way, since it bears on the transparency of your work and on how you are received as a professional when you write. Citation software helps.

Bednarek, Janet R. Daly. 1997. “False Beacon: Regional Planning and the Location of Dayton's Municipal Airport.” Ohio History 106: 125–45.

Corn, Joseph J. 1983. The Winged Gospel: America's Romance with Aviation, 1900–1950. New York: Oxford University Press.

Dahl, Robert A. 1961. Who Governs? Democracy and Power in an American City. New Haven, CT: Yale University Press.

Simmons, Beth A. 2009. Mobilizing for Human Rights: International Law in Domestic Politics. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

Wrap-up

What we learned

  • A research paper moves from the question and why it matters, through what we know and the background, to hypotheses, data, analysis and what the results mean.
  • The abstract gives the question, what we know, the contribution, the data and the conclusions, in that order.
  • The literature review tells readers what work on the exact question says and then shows where our work adds something new.
  • The data section is about transparency, so we say where every measure comes from and defend any that might strike a reader as odd.
  • An analysis interprets its results in real-world terms instead of only reporting numbers.
