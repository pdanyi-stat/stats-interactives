● Practice · Putting it all together

Don't Get Left Behind: Who Got America's First Airports?

In the 1920s, city leaders across the country raced to build municipal airports so their towns would not be left behind in the age of flight. By 1927, 167 cities had a principal airport built or under way. This page applies every technique from Modules 2–11 to one question, which is which U.S. counties got those first airports. These are the data from my 2011 paper, and Step 8 tests its answer, which is that cities whose business leaders, educated elites and civic groups could mobilize built airports first. Each row of the data is a county in 1920, and the notes here are short, with each step linking back to its full module if you need a refresher.

The data

Each row is one U.S. county in 1920, with measures from that year's Census. airport is coded 1 if the county had one of the 167 principal municipal airports built or under way in 1927, and 0 if it did not. The mean of airport is simply the share of counties with an airport. Keep that in mind, because it's what lets us average, test and regress a yes/no outcome. Download the data as airports1920.sav or airports1920.csv.

Step 1 · Modules 2–4: central tendency, skew and dispersion

Describe the counties

A variable's level of measurement decides which average makes sense. For interval-ratio variables, skew decides whether the mean still sits in the middle, and county populations are about as skewed as data get.

Step 2 · Module 5: crosstabs, lambda and gamma

Does knowing X help you guess which counties got an airport?

Airport (the DV) runs down the rows and the IV runs across the columns, and the cells show column percentages. Lambda fits nominal IVs, and gamma fits ordered ones. The strength labels follow the scales from lab.

Step 3 · Module 6: sampling distributions

Pretend the counties are the population, then sample from them

Treat all 1,581 counties as the population, where the true share with an airport is 10.6%. Draw a random sample of counties, record the share with an airport, and then repeat. The sample shares pile up in a bell around the truth, and their spread is the standard error.

Step 4 · Module 7: confidence intervals

Build a confidence interval by hand

The interval is centered on the sample mean. We choose how confident to be, and that choice sets z. The SD and n set the standard error, and everything else is arithmetic.

Step 5 · Module 8: difference in means

Testing which counties got airports

Each test assumes H₀ (no difference) and asks how often a gap this large or larger would show up if H₀ were true. We reject H₀ or fail to reject it, and we never accept anything. Because airport is 0/1, each mean is the share of a group's counties with an airport.

Read first

Regression on a 0/1 outcome goes beyond what we cover in class

In class, our regression DVs are interval-level, like feeling thermometers or vote shares. Airport is only 0 or 1. The mean of a 0/1 variable is a proportion, so the mean of airport is simply the share of counties with an airport. Running ordinary least squares on a 0/1 DV has a name, the linear probability model, and Steps 6–9 use it on purpose as extra illustration. In class and on the labs, regression DVs stay interval-level.

When do social scientists use OLS with binary DVs?

What changes when Y is 0/1

A predicted value ŷ reads as a predicted probability, and a slope reads as a change in percentage points. With a single dummy IV, the slope equals the difference in means from Step 5, exactly.

Its limits

  • Impossible predictions. A straight line can run above 1 or below 0. In Steps 6 and 9, a county with no urban population gets a predicted probability below 0.
  • Bent reality. Near 0 and 1, the true relationship usually curves. Models built for 0/1 outcomes bend the line to follow that curve, and they come in later courses.

How to read Steps 6–9

Interpret every slope as a change in the chance of an airport, in percentage points, and every prediction as a probability. The p-value logic is unchanged, since under H₀ the slope is 0.

Step 6 · Module 9: bivariate regression Linear probability model

One IV at a time

We start with a dummy IV (west) and then a continuous one (percent urban). The equation is the same either way, y = mx + b, or in stats notation f(x) = β₁X₁ + constant.

Step 8 · Module 10: multiple regression · My research question Linear probability model

Did urban regimes build the first airports?

Step 7 showed how much a county's size and urban character explain. These are the data from my 2011 paper, and its question is why some cities built an airport while others that wanted one did not. I drew on urban regime theory (Stone 1989), which holds that city officials work with whoever can bring resources to get things done. If that's right, the cities that built airports should be the ones with business leaders, educated elites and civic groups able to mobilize.

Step 9 · Module 11: dummy variables Linear probability model

Region dummies, then region and percent urban together

Region has four categories, so it gets three dummies. The region left out is the reference, so the constant is its level and each coefficient is a gap from it. Then we add percent urban, and each dummy shifts a line up or down, which gives four parallel lines, one for each region.

Check yourself

The mean of airport is .106. What does that number mean?

10.6% of the counties had an airport built or under way by 1927. For a 0/1 variable, the mean is the share coded 1.

Population has a mean of 56,200 and a median of 27,500. Which describes the typical county?

The median does. A few huge counties, led by Cook County (Chicago) with just over 3 million people, stretch a long right tail (skew 11.7) that drags the mean to twice the median.

For urban group, γ = .85. What does that say?

Moving from the least urban counties to the most urban, the share with an airport rises, and the relationship is strong on the lab scale. Only 0.9% of counties under 25% urban got an airport, against 32.6% of counties over 50% urban.

Northeast vs. Midwest: Sig. = .673. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. The two regions got airports at 11.4% and 10.3%, and a gap that small would turn up often by chance. That isn't evidence the regions were equal, only that there is not enough evidence of a difference.

The slope on west is .103. Where have you seen that number before?

You saw it in Step 5, where it is the West's share with an airport (.196) minus the share elsewhere (.093). A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test, and it gives the same t and p.

The South's slope is −.046 (Sig. = .004) alone but +.019 (Sig. = .216) holding percent urban constant. Why?

Southern counties were less urban (31% on average, against 42% elsewhere), and urban counties got the airports. Alone, the South dummy picked up that rural character. Once percent urban is held constant, a Southern county did about as well as any other county just as urban.

In Step 8, church membership has a slope of .009 per 0.1 (Sig. = .034). What do you conclude?

Reject H₀. Holding population, the other measures and the controls constant, a county with 0.1 more church members per white resident had about a 0.9-point higher chance of an airport. The effect is small but runs the way regime theory predicts, since informal ties help people act together.

Airport counties grew a median 22.3% in the 1920s, against 4.4% for the rest. Did airports cause the growth?

We can't say. The airports came partway through the decade, and the counties that built them were already big, urban and growing. Growth may have brought the airports (reverse causation), and urbanization may have driven both (an omitted variable).

Sources and data
Data: Paul Danyi, "'Don't Get Left Behind': Elites, Urban Regimes, and the Building of America's First Airport Network" (doctoral qualifying paper in public policy, Purdue University, 2011). County measures come from the 1920 U.S. Census (county files from NHGIS, nhgis.org), and church membership comes from the 1926 Census of Religious Bodies. Airports are the 167 "principal" municipal airports built or under way in 1927, as listed in the New York Times ("4,000 Airports Built, But New York City Owns None of Them," pp. 1, 27), with each one coded to its 1920 county. The file covers 1,581 counties in 48 states and D.C., fewer than today's roughly 3,100, because the 1920 county files carry fewer units and some lack data (airports1920.sav, airports1920.csv). The variables south, west and pop3 are made from region and pop1920_k as the variables list shows. Step 3 draws counties at random with replacement, and confidence intervals use z, as in class. Each analysis drops the counties missing any of its variables. Studies comparing linear and logistic regression find the two usually agree on the direction and significance of effects when the outcome's probabilities stay roughly between 20% and 80%, and agree less when the outcome is rare (Hellevik 2009; von Hippel 2017; Gomila 2021). Airports are rare here (10.6% of counties), and the church ratio's slope in Step 8 is significant in the linear model but not in a logistic one (p = .034 vs. .118).

Formulas

Confidence interval for a mean

x‾±z×SDn

z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.58 (99%). For a 0/1 variable, x̄ is a proportion.

Lambda

λ=E1−E2E1

E₁ counts the errors from guessing the DV's mode for everyone, and E₂ counts the errors from guessing each IV column's mode.

Gamma

γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd

Nₛ counts pairs ranked the same way on both variables, and N_d counts pairs ranked in opposite ways. Ties are ignored.

Difference in means (equal variances)

t=x‾1−x‾2SDp1n1+1n2

SDₚ is the pooled SD of the two groups. H₀ says the difference is 0.

Linear probability model

P^(airport)=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+constant

This is ordinary least squares with a 0/1 DV. Each β is a change in the probability of an airport (× 100 for percentage points), holding the other IVs constant.
