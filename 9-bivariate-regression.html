● Module 9 · Regression

Bivariate regression.

A regression line summarizes how Y tends to move as X changes. On this page we will fit that line, read it as an equation, use it to make predictions, and then ask whether its slope is steeper than chance alone would produce.

1 · One case can carry a lot of weight

Recall that r² is the share of the variation in Trump's vote share that the line accounts for. Your version of sits far from where the line would put it, so it adds one very large miss, and r² fell from .36 to . The slope went from −0.77 to .

2 · Where a case sits along X decides its pull

A state near the average income can move a long way up or down without tilting the line much, and on its own it can't cut r² in half here. A state at an extreme income works like a long lever, since the line pivots around the average of X, so moving it both tilts the line and adds a big miss. Move it too far and the slope flips sign, which is why the second goal is there.

3 · Back to the lesson

The slope and r² answer different questions. The slope says how much Trump's share changes with each $1,000 of income, and r² says how closely the states hug the line. A steep slope can sit on top of a weak fit, and one unusual case can change either number, so check the scatterplot for cases like yours before you trust them.

The starting points are the 50 states and the District of Columbia from the income example in panel A, and Trump's 2016 vote shares come from 538's compilation. A state you move becomes your own version and is no longer real data.

BReading the equation

Algebra class
y = mx + b

Here m is the slope, which is the rise over the run, and b is the intercept, the value of y where the line crosses x = 0.

Statistics
ŷ = β₁X₁ + constant

This is the same line with new names. β₁ is the coefficient on the IV, and the constant (Stata calls it _cons, and it is often written β₀) is the intercept. The hat on ŷ tells you the value is “predicted.”

Reading p for a slope. H₀ says the slope is 0, and p is how often a slope at least this far from 0 would turn up by chance if H₀ were true. So p = .03 means about 3 times in 100, and Stata's 0.000 (SPSS prints .000) means p < .001, or fewer than 1 time in 1,000. If p < .05 we reject H₀, and otherwise we fail to reject it. The .05 cutoff is a convention we choose, and it sets our risk of a Type I error (a false positive). Reading p-values → · Type I and II errors →

Check yourself

      

        In the House example, the slope on PVI is about 0.99. Interpret it.
        
Each 1-point increase in a district's Democratic lean (its Cook PVI) is associated with about a 0.99-point increase in the Democratic candidate's predicted share of the two-party vote. We say “associated with” rather than “causes,” since this is one variable in observational data.

      

      

        The constant is 56.8. Is that a meaningful prediction?
        
Yes. A PVI of 0 marks a district that leans toward neither party, and 33 districts sit within 2 points of it, so x = 0 is well inside the observed range. The constant predicts that a Democrat running in such a district won 56.8% of the two-party vote in 2026. The constant is only a meaningful prediction when x = 0 is a realistic value. In the metro share example, no state comes near a metro share of 0, so its constant of 77 predicts nothing real.

      

      

        Why does the line always pass through (x̄, ȳ)?
        
Because the constant is computed as ȳ − β₁x̄. Plug x̄ into the equation and you get exactly ȳ. That is why panel D can pivot every line around the mean point.

      

      

        Stata reports t = b / SE. What does a t of about 76 mean in panel D?
        
The real slope is about 76 standard errors away from zero, so far outside the wedge that the shuffled slopes look like a thin sliver around 0. A slope that steep would essentially never come from shuffled, unrelated data, so p is tiny, and the output prints it as 0.000.

      

      

        Can a line be steep but not significant?
        
Yes. Significance depends on the slope relative to its standard error. With few cases or lots of scatter, the bell of chance slopes is wide, and even a steep line can fall inside the wedge.

      

    

  


  
  
Wrap-up
What we learned

  

    
    
      
  • The least-squares line is the line with the smallest sum of squared errors.
    • 
      
  • The slope tells us how much Y is predicted to change for a one-unit change in X, and the constant is the predicted Y when X is 0.
    • 
      
  • We use the equation to predict Y at values of X that readers can relate to, such as the average case and the most extreme case.
    • 
      
  • The t test on the slope asks whether a slope this steep could come from chance alone. If p is below .05 we reject H₀ that the true slope is 0.

Formulas

  
The model

    y^=β1X1+constant⟺y=mx+b
    
Many textbooks write the constant as β₀ and put it first: ŷ = β₀ + β₁X₁.

  
Least-squares slope and constant

    β1=∑(xi−x‾)(yi−y‾)∑(xi−x‾)2constant=y‾−β1x‾
    
These values minimize the sum of squared residuals, SSE = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)².

  
Predicted change (marginal effect)

    Δy^=β1·Δx
    
For a straight line the change depends only on how far X moves rather than where it starts. Rise = slope × run.

  
Fit

    r=∑(xi−x‾)(yi−y‾)∑(xi−x‾)2∑(yi−y‾)2r2=1−SSESST

  
Standard error of the slope and the t test

    SE(β1)=SSE/(n−2)∑(xi−x‾)2t=β1−0SE(β1)
    
More scatter around the line (a bigger SSE) widens the bell of chance slopes, while more spread in X narrows it. Under H₀, where β₁ = 0, t follows a t distribution with n − 2 degrees of freedom. The 95% confidence interval is β₁ ± t*·SE.

  
Why the bell lands on the right edge

    yedge=y‾+b·(xedge−x‾)
    
Each line through (x̄, ȳ) reaches the right edge at a height that is a straight-line function of its slope b. So the distribution of slopes becomes a distribution of heights on that edge, which is the same bell curve turned 90°.
