Find the median by putting the values in order

The median is the value with as many observations below it as above it. With an even number of observations, it is halfway between the two middle values.

Part 4

Find the median in a frequency table

The median is the answer given by the middle person once everyone is lined up in order, which here runs from “Increased a lot” to “Decreased a lot.” With thousands of people we don't line them up by hand. Instead we read the Cumulative Percent column, which adds up the Valid Percent as you go down the rows. The median is the first row where the cumulative percent reaches 50%, because that row holds the middle person.

Three levels of measurement

Part 12

Ordinal variables: the mean of the codes misleads

The categories now have a real order, so the median works. The codes stored in the data file are still only one step apart, so the mean depends on how we happened to number the categories.

Part 13

Ordinal codes hide unequal gaps

Ordinal codes are always one step apart, but the categories they stand for aren't always the same distance apart. Pick an example, then tap the bottom line to see where a gap is larger or smaller than one step, and why.

Part 14

Interval variables: the mode and the mean need care

With real numbers the mean and the median both make sense. The mode depends on how finely we round the values, and a long tail pulls the mean away from the typical case.

Part 15

Range, and why bar charts don't suit interval variables

The range runs from the lowest observed value to the highest. Feeling thermometers run from 0 (cold) to 100 (warm).

Confidence in institutions in 71 countries (confidence)

Here is an example from a cross-national dataset. It scores 71 countries on how much confidence their publics have in the country's institutions on a scale from 0 (none) to 100 (complete). It's an interval variable, and the scores have decimals, for example 47.97 or 52.31.

A bar chart gives every distinct value its own bar, and each bar's height is the number of cases with exactly that value. That works when a variable has a handful of categories, like religion or passenger class, because many cases share each value. On a 0–100 scale measured to two decimals, almost every country has a value of its own, so a bar chart just lines up 71 bars of the same height. A histogram groups the scores into ranges first, and then counts how many countries fall in each range.

Wrap-up

What we learned

  • The mode is the most common value, the median is the middle value once the values are in order, and the mean is the sum of the values divided by the number of cases.
  • Nominal variables only name categories, ordinal variables put categories in order with unequal gaps, and interval variables have an order and equal gaps.
  • The mode works at every level, the median needs at least an ordinal variable, and the mean needs an interval variable.
  • The mean of a nominal variable's codes names no category, and the mean of an ordinal variable's codes changes when we renumber the categories, even though no case changes category.
  • For interval variables, the mode depends on how finely we group the values, and a long tail pulls the mean away from the typical case, so the median is often the safer summary.
  • Dummy variables (coded 0 and 1) are a special case where the mode, the median and the mean all work, and the mean is the share of cases coded 1.
