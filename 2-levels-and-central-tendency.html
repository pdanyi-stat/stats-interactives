Levels of measurement and central tendency.

Part 1

Sort variables by level of measurement

Nominal variables only name a category. Ordinal variables put categories in order, but the gaps between them aren't equal. Interval variables have an order and equal gaps.

Part 3

Find the median by putting the values in order

The median is the value with as many observations below it as above it. With an even number of observations, it is halfway between the two middle values.

Put the smallest on the left and the largest on the right. Drag a tile, or tap two tiles to swap them. Arrow keys also move a focused tile.

Part 4

The mean is the balance point

Put a weight on a beam at each county's value. The point where the beam balances is the mean. Slide the triangle until the beam is level.

Lunatics per county, Massachusetts, 1854

Each blue weight is one of the 13 counties. The number on it is the county's count of lunatics (variable NBR, “Lunatics Per County”), and its line drops to that value on the beam, which is marked like a ruler. Weights at nearly the same value stack up. Drag on the chart or use the slider to move the triangle. “Lunatics” is the dataset's term, which comes from the era.

Now move Suffolk County (Boston) further out

Suffolk has the most lunatics, 458. Drag the slider to give it more. The triangle stays under the mean, so it slides right with Suffolk. The median (blue marker) doesn't move, because Suffolk is still the largest county either way.

Part 5

Why the mean of a nominal variable means nothing

The numbers SPSS stores for a nominal variable are only labels. You can put the categories in any order and number them any way you like, and nothing is lost, because every country keeps its religion and every religion keeps its count. A measure that changes when you rearrange the categories is reacting to the labels rather than to the countries.

Part 8

Find the median in a frequency table

The median is the answer given by the middle person once everyone is lined up in order, which here runs from “Increased a lot” to “Decreased a lot.” With thousands of people we don't line them up by hand. Instead we read the Cumulative Percent column, which adds up the Valid Percent as you go down the rows. The median is the first row where the cumulative percent reaches 50%, because that row holds the middle person.

Part 9

Range, and why bar charts don't suit interval variables

The range runs from the lowest observed value to the highest. Feeling thermometers run from 0 (cold) to 100 (warm).

The range is a first look at dispersion, which measures how spread out a variable is. Module 4 covers dispersion in full, and the bar-chart point here previews how we will graph interval variables there.

How do you feel about Southerners? (southern_therm, 2004 ANES)

Confidence in institutions in 71 countries (confidence)

Here is an example from a cross-national dataset. It scores 71 countries on how much confidence their publics have in the country's institutions on a scale from 0 (none) to 100 (complete). It's an interval variable, and the scores have decimals, for example 47.97 or 52.31.

A bar chart gives every distinct value its own bar, and each bar's height is the number of cases with exactly that value. That works when a variable has a handful of categories, like religion or passenger class, because many cases share each value. On a 0–100 scale measured to two decimals, almost every country has a value of its own, so a bar chart just lines up 71 bars of the same height. A histogram groups the scores into ranges first, and then counts how many countries fall in each range.
