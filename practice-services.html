Who wants the government to do more?

This page applies every technique from Modules 2–11 to one question from the 2024 American National Election Study, which is who wants the government to provide more services. Unlike the Titanic and voting pages, the outcome here is a seven-point scale rather than a yes/no, so the regressions in Steps 6–8 are the kind we run in class. The notes here are short, and each step links back to its full module if you need a refresher.

The data

Each row is one respondent who placed themselves on the ANES services scale. The question asks people to choose between two positions. At one end, the government should provide many fewer services, even in areas such as health and education, in order to reduce spending. At the other end, it should provide many more services, even if it means an increase in spending. services runs from 1 = many fewer services to 7 = many more services, so a higher score means wanting the government to do more.

Step 1 · Modules 2–4: central tendency, skew and dispersion

Describe the respondents

A variable's level of measurement decides which average makes sense. For interval-ratio variables, skew decides whether the mean still sits in the middle.

Step 2 · Modules 4b and 5: crosstabs, lambda and gamma

Does knowing X help you guess who wants more services?

For a crosstab, we group the seven points into fewer services (1–3), the middle (4) and more services (5–7). The services groups (the DV) run down the rows and the IV runs across the columns, and the cells show column percentages. Lambda fits nominal IVs, and gamma fits ordered ones.

Step 3 · Module 6: sampling distributions

Pretend the respondents are the population, then sample from them

Treat all 4,751 respondents as the population, where the true mean on the services scale is 4.53. Draw a random sample of respondents, record its mean, and then repeat. The sample means pile up in a bell around the truth, and their spread is the standard error.

Step 4 · Module 7: confidence intervals

Build a confidence interval by hand

The interval is centered on the sample mean. We choose how confident to be, and that choice sets z. The SD and n set the standard error, and everything else is arithmetic.

Refresh: Module 7 →

Step 5 · Module 8: difference in means

Testing who wants more

Each test assumes H₀ (no difference) and asks how often a gap this large or larger would show up if H₀ were true. We reject H₀ or fail to reject it, and we never accept anything.

Read first

A seven-point scale as the DV

Strictly, the services scale is ordinal, since it has seven ordered points and nothing guarantees that the steps between them are equal. Researchers still commonly treat a scale like this one as interval-level and run regression on it, as we do with feeling thermometers, because its points are evenly spaced on the page and only the ends carry labels. Read every slope in Steps 6–8 as a change in scale points, where 1 point is one step along the seven-point line.

Step 6 · Module 9: bivariate regression

One IV at a time

We start with a dummy IV (female) and then a continuous one (age). The equation is the same either way, y = mx + b, or in stats notation f(x) = β₁X₁ + constant.

Step 7 · Module 10: multiple regression

College, holding party constant

On its own, a college degree seems to make no difference to how many services people want. Add controls and watch its slope change. All four models use the same respondents, those with a known party, education and age, so the comparison is fair.

Refresh: Module 10 →

Step 8 · Module 11: dummy variables

Party dummies, then party, sex and age together

Party has three categories, so it gets two dummies. The category left out is the reference, so the constant is its mean and each coefficient is a gap from it. Then we add female and age, and each dummy shifts a line up or down, which gives six parallel lines, one for each combination of party and sex.

Check yourself

The mean of services is 4.53. What does that number mean?

On average, respondents sit about half a point above the middle of the scale (4), so the typical respondent leans slightly toward wanting more services, even if it means more spending.

Democrats average 5.45 and Republicans 3.48. Interpret the gap.

Democrats sit about two scale points closer to “many more services” than Republicans, which is about a third of the six-point distance from one end of the scale to the other. That is the largest gap among the groups on this page.

No college vs. college: Sig. = .479. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. On their own, respondents with and without a bachelor's degree want about the same level of services. That isn't evidence the groups are the same, only that there is not enough evidence of a difference.

The slope on female is .555. Where have you seen that number before?

You saw it in Step 5, where it is women's mean (4.80) minus men's (4.25). A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test, and it gives the same t and p.

College's slope is .037 (Sig. = .479) alone but negative and significant holding party constant. Why?

College graduates are more often Democrats, and Democrats want more services. Leaving party out let college borrow the Democrats' higher scores, which hid the fact that, within each party, college graduates want somewhat fewer services.

Sources and data
Data: American National Election Studies, 2024 Time Series Study, pre-election interview. The course file (ANES_2024_Services_F26.sav) holds the original variables and the recodes services, party3, ideo, age, agegrp, educ5, college, income6, income3, race, female, region and south. The page uses the 4,751 respondents who placed themselves on the services scale (V241239). People who said they hadn't thought much about it, didn't know or refused are left out. Counts are unweighted, as in lab, so they describe these respondents rather than all American adults, and the page treats them as a sample to practice inference. Step 3 draws respondents at random with replacement. Confidence intervals use z, as in class. Models drop respondents missing on any variable in the model.

Formulas

Confidence interval for a mean

x‾±z×SDn

z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.58 (99%).

Lambda

λ=E1−E2E1

E₁ counts the errors from guessing the DV's mode for everyone, and E₂ counts the errors from guessing each IV column's mode.

Gamma

γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd

Nₛ counts pairs ranked the same way on both variables, and N_d counts pairs ranked in opposite ways. Ties are ignored.

Difference in means (equal variances)

t=x‾1−x‾2SDp1n1+1n2

SDₚ is the pooled SD of the two groups. H₀ says the difference is 0.

Multiple regression

y^=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+constant

Each β is the predicted change in services for a one-unit change in that X, holding the other IVs constant.
