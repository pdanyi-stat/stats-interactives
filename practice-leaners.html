Are leaners partisans?

About a third of Americans call themselves independents when they are first asked about their party, but most of them then say they lean toward one. This page applies every technique from Modules 2–11 to one question about those leaners, which is whether they feel about the parties like independents or like partisans. The data come from the 2024 American National Election Study, and each step links back to its full module if you need a refresher.

The data

Each row is one respondent. The ANES first asks whether you think of yourself as a Democrat, a Republican or an independent. Partisans are then asked whether they identify strongly or not very strongly, and independents are asked whether they feel closer to one of the parties. Leaners are the independents who name a party. Our DV, thermgap, is how far apart a respondent rates the two parties on the 0–100 feeling thermometers, so it is 0 for someone who rates them the same and 100 for someone who loves one party and hates the other.

Step 1 · Modules 2–4: central tendency, skew and dispersion

Describe the respondents

A variable's level of measurement decides which average makes sense. For interval-ratio variables, skew decides whether the mean still sits in the middle.

Step 2 · Modules 4b and 5: crosstabs, lambda and gamma

Does knowing X help you guess how far apart people rate the parties?

For a crosstab, we group thermgap into a small gap (0–24), a middle gap (25–74) and a large gap (75–100). The gap groups (the DV) run down the rows and the IV runs across the columns, and the cells show column percentages. Lambda fits nominal IVs, and gamma fits ordered ones.

Step 3 · Module 6: sampling distributions

Pretend the respondents are the population, then sample from them

Treat all 5,450 respondents who rated both parties as the population, where the true mean thermgap is 51.2. Draw a random sample of respondents, record its mean, and then repeat. The sample means pile up in a bell around the truth, and their spread is the standard error.

Step 4 · Module 7: confidence intervals

Build a confidence interval by hand

The interval is centered on the sample mean. We choose how confident to be, and that choice sets z. The SD and n set the standard error, and everything else is arithmetic.

Step 5 · Module 8: difference in means

Testing the leaners

Each test assumes H₀ (no difference) and asks how often a gap this large or larger would show up if H₀ were true. We reject H₀ or fail to reject it, and we never accept anything.

Read first

Why the distance between the thermometers?

Democratic leaners rate the Democratic Party warmly and Republican leaners rate it coldly, so on one thermometer their scores would cancel out. The distance between the two thermometers puts both kinds of partisans on one scale, since it asks only how far apart a person rates the parties. Read every slope in Steps 6–8 as a change in thermometer points, where 100 points is the whole distance between rating the parties the same and loving one while hating the other.

Step 6 · Module 9: bivariate regression

One IV at a time

We start with a dummy IV (strong partisan or not) and then a continuous one (age). The equation is the same either way, y = mx + b, or in stats notation f(x) = β₁X₁ + constant.

Step 7 · Module 10: multiple regression

Leaners and weak partisans, holding age constant

On their own, leaners and weak partisans can't be told apart. Add controls and watch the leaner slope change. Every model here uses the same leaners and weak partisans, those with a known age and attention, so the comparison is fair.

Step 8 · Module 11: dummy variables

Strength dummies, then strength, sex and age together

Party strength has four categories, so it gets three dummies. The category left out is the reference, so the constant is its mean and each coefficient is a gap from it. Then we add female and age, and each dummy shifts a line up or down, which gives eight parallel lines, one for each combination of strength and sex.

Check yourself

The mean thermgap is 51.2. What does that number mean?

On average, respondents rate the two parties about 51 points apart on the 0–100 thermometers, so the typical American feels much warmer toward one party than the other.

Leaners vs. weak partisans: Sig. = .119. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. A gap as small as this one would turn up often by chance if leaners and weak partisans rated the parties equally far apart. That isn't evidence the two groups are the same, only that the data give no evidence of a difference.

The slope on strong is 32.2. Where have you seen that number before?

You saw it in Step 5, where it is the strong partisans' mean minus everyone else's. A regression on one dummy is the equal-variance t test, and it gives the same t and p.

The leaner slope is −1.7 alone but −2.5 holding age constant. Why?

Leaners are older on average than weak partisans, and older people rate the parties farther apart. Leaving age out let leaners borrow older people's wider gaps, which hid the fact that, at the same age, leaners rate the parties slightly closer together than weak partisans do.

With pure independents as the reference, the leaner coefficient is 27.2. What does it mean?

On average, leaners rate the two parties about 27 points farther apart than pure independents do, which puts them far closer to the weak partisans than to the independents.

Sources and data
Data: American National Election Studies, 2024 Time Series Study, pre-election interview (classroom extract, NES_2024_Party_Placement_and_Ideology_F26.sav). The page uses the 5,483 respondents with a valid party ID, and tests that use thermgap leave out the 33 who did not rate both parties. The course file adds thermgap and pidstrength, computed from V241166, V241167 and V241227x, and merges age, agegrp, female, college and attention from the full 2024 study by case ID (V240001). Counts are unweighted, as in lab, and the page treats the respondents as a sample to practice inference. Step 3 draws respondents at random with replacement. Confidence intervals use z, as in class. Formulas

Confidence interval for a mean

x‾±z×SDn

z = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.58 (99%).

Lambda

λ=E1−E2E1

E₁ counts the errors from guessing the DV's mode for everyone, and E₂ counts the errors from guessing each IV column's mode.

Gamma

γ=Ns−NdNs+Nd

Nₛ counts pairs ranked the same way on both variables, and N_d counts pairs ranked in opposite ways. Ties are ignored.

Difference in means (equal variances)

t=x‾1−x‾2SDp1n1+1n2

SDₚ is the pooled SD of the two groups. H₀ says the difference is 0.

Multiple regression

y^=β1X1+β2X2+⋯+constant

Each β is the predicted change in thermgap for a one-unit change in that X, holding the other IVs constant.
