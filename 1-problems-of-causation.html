● Module 1 · From correlation to cause

Problems of causation.

When two variables move together, we are tempted to tell a story in which one causes the other. This module covers what it takes to call a relationship causal, and then the common ways a correlation can lead us to the wrong story.

Part 1

When does a correlation suggest a cause?

A correlation is the presence of covariation between two variables, meaning that as the values on one variable change, the values on the second change systematically with it. You have likely heard that correlation does not imply causation. In fact a correlation often suggests a cause quite strongly, so our job is to ask whether that suggestion holds up once we start questioning it.

Social science as storytelling

Theories specify general relationships between things we observe, and a good theory tells us not only that two things are related but how they are related. That "how" is a mechanism (some plausible, and preferably observed, process that runs between one variable and the outcome). Mechanisms are often the stories we tell between our variables to explain something, and good research gathers evidence that makes us less uncertain about those stories.

Social scientists use the language of mechanisms in qualitative and quantitative work alike. Alexander George and Andrew Bennett (2005) describe causal mechanisms as stable factors that, under certain conditions, link causes to effects, and they argue that mechanisms matter as much to a causal explanation as the size of an effect does. Case studies allow close examination of the social mechanisms that may underlie causation (Janssen Danyi and Janssen Danyi 2024), and case study researchers look for those mechanisms through process tracing, which follows the links between possible causes and observed outcomes through histories, documents and interviews. Henry Brady and David Collier (2010) call this kind of evidence a causal-process observation (an insight or piece of data about context, process or mechanism), and they argue that it belongs alongside the rows and columns of a quantitative data set.

Statistical work and storytelling depend on each other. A regression can tell us how much Y changes with X, but case studies remain stronger at showing whether and how a variable mattered (George and Bennett 2005, 25), and experiments, which can rarely take whole organizations or societies as their cases, test an average human response that we then look for in the messier world outside the lab (Janssen Danyi and Janssen Danyi 2024). Every problem on this page is, at bottom, a bad story. The correlation is real, but the story we tell between X and Y is wrong, and the fix is a better story backed by better evidence.

Four requirements for calling a relationship causal

  1. The cause must come before the effect (temporal priority).
  2. The relationship must be non-spurious, which means it cannot be random or accidental.
  3. We must eliminate rival explanations for what produces the correlation.
  4. We need a mechanism, a plausible and preferably observed story that connects the cause to the outcome.

Pick a claim, then tap each requirement to see whether the claim meets it.

Part 2

Spurious relationships (X → ? → Y)

A spurious relationship is one in which two things correlate without any reasonable mechanism between them. The world produces a great many patterns by pure accident, so if a story connecting the two is nearly impossible to tell, the relationship might be spurious.

How to manufacture a spurious correlation

Imagine a reporter with 20 years of yearly records for one state. The records cover 40 things that have nothing to do with one another, such as movie tickets sold, inches of snowfall and pumpkins harvested. The records here are simulated, and in the simulation each one drifts up or down by chance from one year to the next, so we know for certain that none of them affects any other. The reporter then goes looking for a story.

  1. The reporter lines up every possible pair of records. With 40 records there are 780 pairs.
  2. The reporter computes r for each pair. Most pairs land near zero, but with 780 tries a few land far from zero by luck alone.
  3. The reporter writes up the pair that matches most closely and leaves the other 779 out of the story.

Omitted variables (Z → X and Y)

Sometimes the story we tell between X and Y is wrong because X and Y share a common cause, Z, that drives each of them. These relationships often look spurious until the common cause is revealed.

Part 4

Endogeneity (X ⇄ Y)

With endogeneity, the story we tell from X to Y is wrong because X and Y are locked in a feedback loop, where one has influenced the other in the past and the other has pushed back (the slides give it a less polite name). We risk endogeneity problems whenever we lack information about which thing came first.

Happiness and leisure

Countries where people have more leisure time also tend to be happier. State the relationship in one direction first, and then in the other. Once you have tried both, you can run them together.

Does campaign money help incumbents?

In US House elections, Gary Jacobson found that the challenger's spending was strongly related to the vote, while the incumbent's spending showed a relationship that was weak, near zero, or even in the wrong direction. Taken at face value, that would mean money does almost nothing for incumbents.

As with leisure and happiness, state this relationship in one direction first, and then in the other.

The trouble is that incumbents raise and spend more when they see a strong challenger coming. A simple correlation can't pull those two arrows apart, which is why the effect of incumbent spending is still argued over.

Part 5

Intervening variables (X → Z → Y)

Here the story between X and Y goes wrong because it skips a step. The missing step is a mechanism, present in most cases, that carries the influence of X to Y. These relationships often look reasonable, but the story seems to have a stretch in it.

Youth and left-party voting

Age is strongly related to political ideology, and youthful voters disproportionately favor left parties. Is being young itself the cause? Tap the button to fill in the chain one step at a time and watch the explanatory gaps close.

Celebrity suicides and total suicides

Celebrity suicides correlate with the total number of suicides in a country. Can we conclude that the death of a celebrity causes people to take their own lives at higher rates, or does the story need more?

The missing step is how the death is reported. Sociologists have studied this link for half a century. David Phillips (1974) examined suicide rates in the US and Britain from 1947 to 1968 and found that suicides rose after highly publicized suicide stories. He named the pattern the Werther effect, after the hero of Goethe's 1774 novel The Sorrows of Young Werther.

More recently, a 2020 review of 31 studies in the BMJ (Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues) found that suicides rose by 13% on average in the weeks after the media reported a celebrity's death by suicide. The influence runs through the coverage, which is why suicide prevention groups publish guidelines for how journalists should report these deaths.

The research also points to what in the coverage carries the influence. In the same 2020 review, when the media reported the method a celebrity used, deaths by that same method rose by 30%. Coverage does more than spread the news of a death, because it can also pass along information about the means. That is why reporting guidelines ask journalists not to describe how someone died.

The same step explains why doctors and mental health professionals ask a person in crisis whether they have made a plan. The National Institute of Mental Health's screening guide tells clinicians to ask about a plan, including the method and access to the means, and it treats a detailed, workable plan as more serious than thoughts that haven't been worked out. A plan and the means to carry it out are the links between distress and an attempt, so they are where clinicians, and careful reporting, try to break the chain.

Part 6

Definition problems (X = Y)

Sometimes the story fails because X is not really distinct from Y. We risk this often when we look for relationships between a big, multifaceted concept and one of the things that make it up.

Part 7

Reverse causation (Y → X)

Reverse causation means the story we tell misses that Y is actually the cause of X. These claims can look reasonable, but they often lack a clear statement of when X happened in relation to Y. They can look like endogeneity problems, but they differ in that we can eventually identify which came first and diagnose the problem.

Do police cause crime?

Steven Levitt pointed out that Newark had a violent crime rate four times higher than Omaha's and also had twice as many police per capita. The bars below put each city on a scale where Omaha equals 1.

Time in the district and impending retirement

The time a representative spends in their district correlates with impending retirement, and members who are about to leave also miss more roll-call votes in Washington. Does spending more time at home lead a member to retire? Step through the final term of a hypothetical member and watch the order in which things happen.

Research on this "last-period problem" backs up the order. John Lott (1987) and David Figlio (1995) both found that House members miss more roll-call votes once they have decided to leave, and Figlio estimated that retiring members took part in just over 8 percentage points fewer roll calls in their final term than they had before it.

Part 8

Reversion to the mean

Every golfer, team or person has a usual level, and any single result mixes that level with luck. An extreme result is usually extreme partly because of luck, and luck doesn't repeat, so the next result tends to fall back toward that golfer's or team's own usual level. That is reversion (or regression) to the mean. It becomes a causal trap when we step in at an extreme moment, because whatever we do then gets the credit for a recovery that was coming anyway.

Two rounds of golf

At a big golf tournament every player plays a first round and then a second round on the same course, and lower scores are better. The 156 golfers below are simulated, after a figure in Bueno de Mesquita and Fowler's Thinking Clearly with Data (2021) that uses real first- and second-round scores from the U.S. Women's Open. Each simulated golfer has her own usual score, and each round adds luck to it.

Each golfer starts on the dashed 45-degree line, which is where she would land if her second round matched her first. When round 2 plays, watch where the golfers with the best and the worst first rounds go.

The policing problem

The membership drive

Suppose you lead a student group that usually has about 20 members, and one year an unusually large membership drive brings it to 40. Two years later, in your senior year, the group is down to 18 and doesn't recover much. Your co-leaders say tastes have changed, but the likelier story is that 40 was the extreme year and the group has returned to its usual size.

The magazine-cover jinx

Athletes who appear on the cover of Sports Illustrated often have a worse season next. As Daniel Kahneman explains in Thinking, Fast and Slow, they made the cover after an exceptional season that was probably helped along by luck, and luck doesn't last. This hypothetical hitter's cover season (.338) sits well above her career average of .290, and the next season (.291) falls back to it.

We can guard against reversion in two ways. First, we remember that it exists and that one of the results we are comparing may simply be a lucky or unlucky extreme. Second, we think about how cases come to be treated in the real world, since cases chosen because they are extreme will tend to look better afterward whatever we do to them. The fair comparison is with similar cases that hit the same extreme and were left alone.
