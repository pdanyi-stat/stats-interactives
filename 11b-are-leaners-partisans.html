Are leaners partisans?

About a third of Americans call themselves independents when they are first asked about their party. Most of them, when asked one more question, say they lean toward a party. Are these leaners really independents, or are they partisans by another name? This worked example tests that question with the 2024 American National Election Study.

The data

The ANES first asks whether you think of yourself as a Democrat, a Republican or an independent. Democrats and Republicans are then asked whether they identify with their party strongly or not very strongly, and independents are asked whether they feel closer to one of the parties. Those answers build the seven-point party ID scale (V241227x), which runs from 1 = Strong Democrat to 7 = Strong Republican. Leaners are the 3s and 5s, who first said “independent” and then named a party.

Step 1 · Counting

How many Americans are independents?

The answer depends on where we put the leaners. Switch between the two ways of counting and watch the independents grow or shrink.

Step 2 · Always look at your data

Do leaners feel like partisans?

If leaners were true independents, they should rate the two parties about the same and sit near the middle on ideology, like the pure independents. If they are partisans, they should look like the weak identifiers next to them.

Step 3 · Module 8: difference in means

Test it with the distance between the two thermometers

To put every respondent on one scale, we take the Democratic rating minus the Republican rating and drop the sign. This distance (thermgap) is 0 for someone who rates the parties the same and 100 for someone who loves one and hates the other. Then we compare the groups with t tests.

What we can conclude

The thermometers measure feelings, not behavior. Keith and his co-authors (1992) found the same pattern for how leaners vote, but this classroom file has no vote choice, so we cannot test that part here.

Check yourself

Leaners vs. weak partisans: Sig. = .119. What do you conclude?

Fail to reject H₀. A gap as small as this one would turn up often by chance if leaners and weak partisans rated the parties equally far apart. That isn't proof the two groups are the same, only that the data give no evidence of a difference.

Why use the distance between the two thermometers rather than the Democratic thermometer alone?

Democratic leaners rate the Democratic Party warmly and Republican leaners rate it coldly, so their scores on one thermometer would cancel out. The distance puts both kinds of partisans on one scale, since it asks only how far apart a person rates the two parties.

With pure independents as the reference, the leaner coefficient is 27.2. What does it mean?

On average, leaners rate the two parties about 27 points farther apart than pure independents do.

Why does it matter whether we count leaners as independents?

Counting them as independents makes about a third of the public look unattached to the parties. Counting them with their parties leaves only about 7% who are pure independents, and Steps 2–4 show that leaners feel about the parties the way weak partisans do.

Notes on the data

American National Election Studies, 2024 Time Series, pre-election interview (classroom extract, NES_2024_Party_Placement_and_Ideology_F26.sav). The page uses the 5,483 respondents with a valid party ID. Respondents who refused or did not know a thermometer rating are left out of the tests that use it, and the ideology view leaves out people who said they hadn't thought much about ideology. The variables thermgap and pidstrength are computed from V241166, V241167 and V241227x, and the course file includes them. Counts are unweighted, as in lab.
